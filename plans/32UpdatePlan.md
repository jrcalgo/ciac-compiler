# CIaC v0.32-file — Spending the Instrument: Twelve Optimizations, Each Answerable to a Number (implementation plan)

> Implementation plan. Document number ≠ release number (standing
> precedent; expected to ship as **0.30.0**). Assumes
> `26UpdatePlan.md`–`31UpdatePlan.md` shipped, the last of them as
> 0.29.0. This arc is the one `31UpdatePlan.md` was built to make
> possible, and it is the direct inverse of it.
>
> That arc was **instrument-only**. It shipped `ciac-bench`, the
> committed and environment-stamped `docs/perf/baseline.json`,
> `perf_baseline.rs` (callgrind instruction counts), `perf_scaling.rs`
> (growth ratios), `scripts/bench-verify.sh`,
> `scripts/bench-callgrind.sh`, and a written noise-floor study — and
> it changed *no compiler behavior whatsoever*, deliberately, under a
> contract stated in its own preamble. Its closing table mapped a
> ranked inventory of twelve optimizations against the instruments
> that would measure each. Every row got an instrument. **Not one row
> got an implementation.**
>
> This arc implements all twelve. Every milestone is a measured
> before/after against the committed baseline, with a threshold
> pre-registered in this document *before* that milestone's code
> exists, and with rollback as a first-class, pre-registered outcome
> rather than an embarrassment discovered at review time.
>
> **Contract 1 — byte identity.** Every generated byte, on every
> target, for every example, is identical before and after this arc.
> This is a *stronger* claim than `31UpdatePlan.md`'s: that arc
> promised not to touch the compiler, so byte identity was trivially
> implied. This arc touches `ciac-codegen`, `ciac-ir`, and `ciac`'s
> own CLI in nearly every milestone, and still promises the output
> cannot tell. The enforcement mechanism is unchanged and is not
> modified by this arc: the 145 golden snapshots
> (`tests/tests/snapshots/golden__gen__{python,rust,typescript,go,java}__*.snap`),
> `determinism.rs`, `conformance.rs`, `openapi.rs`, `golden.rs`, the
> `tests/ui/` negative corpus, and `scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh` — 50
> program×target combinations that *execute* generated code rather
> than merely diffing it. If any of these moves, the milestone stops
> and the cause is investigated as a defect before anything else
> proceeds. There is no disclosed exception anywhere in this arc: any
> snapshot movement at all is a bug.
>
> **Contract 2 — no metric may regress to buy another.** A milestone
> that improves its own named metric while pushing another outside its
> noise band is a *trade*, not a win, and the trade must be stated and
> accepted explicitly at that milestone rather than discovered three
> milestones later. `ciac-bench --compare` reports every metric it
> knows about, not only the one a milestone targets, precisely so that
> a silent trade cannot hide in the part of the report nobody was
> looking at. This contract is why the full suite runs at every
> milestone boundary (Pillar 3) rather than only at the arc's close.
>
> **Contract 3 — rollback is graduated, and pre-registered.** Revert on
> any correctness failure, any generated-byte change, any metric
> regression outside its noise band, or a measured gain under **half**
> its pre-registered threshold. Between half and full threshold: keep
> the change and record the shortfall explicitly, in the milestone
> report and in this document's own running log. A reverted milestone
> is a *result* of this arc, not a failure of it — precisely the
> posture `31UpdatePlan.md` M5 took toward dropping a gate outright,
> and `30UpdatePlan.md` M5 took toward stopping an arc early.
>
> **Confidence:** high on items 1, 2 and 4 — each is a small,
> mechanically verifiable change with a narrow blast radius and an
> already-measured price tag. Moderate on item 3: the eager clones are
> confirmed in source, but how much of the measured allocation total
> they represent is unknown until M7's DHAT run. Moderate-to-low on
> item 10: the diagnosis is structurally unambiguous — `SystemGraph`
> is bare `Vec`s with literally zero indexes — but the risk is not in
> the fix, it is in the *audit*, because a mutation path missed when
> adding an index produces a wrong lookup rather than a crash, and a
> wrong lookup is a correctness bug wearing a performance patch's
> clothes. Genuinely unknown on item 11: the Java formatter's cost may
> be an unmovable JVM-startup floor, and this document says so before
> finding out rather than after. Every number quoted below was
> measured during `31UpdatePlan.md`'s own execution, on this machine
> (4 vCPU, 16 GB, no SHA-NI) — **ratios are expected to travel to
> other hardware; absolute timings are not**, and no threshold in this
> document is stated as an absolute for that reason.

### What was verified in source before this plan was written

A plan built on a profile nobody re-read is exactly the failure mode
`31UpdatePlan.md` was written to prevent — its own M9 retrospective
recorded a documentation table that went stale because a number lived
somewhere nobody revisited. So before any threshold below was chosen,
each headline claim was checked against the actual code rather than
re-quoted from the motivating investigation. Four were confirmed, and
one was confirmed *smaller than claimed*, which is recorded here
rather than quietly rounded up.

**Item 2 is real, and it is precisely two passes over generated
content, not three.** `crates/ciac-codegen/src/regen.rs:104-106`, inside
`plan_regeneration`, computes both `disk_hash` (over bytes read from
disk) and `new_hash = hash_content(generated)` (over the freshly
generated string). `crates/ciac-codegen/src/manifest.rs:118`, inside
`build_manifest`, then calls `hash_content(content)` again over the
*same* generated string, on the same build, having been handed the
same `GeneratedProject`. The disk-hash pass is load-bearing — it is
the entire mechanism by which `Update`, `Conflict` and `SeededDrift`
are distinguished from `Unchanged` — so only the generated-content
hash is doubled. The motivating investigation described "2–3 hash
passes"; the accurate count is two over generated content plus one
over disk content, of which exactly one is removable. M2's threshold
is set accordingly, and deliberately below the 15.09% that
`31UpdatePlan.md` M6's deliberate-regression experiment measured for
*adding* a third pass.

**Item 3 is unconditional cloning, at two exact lines.**
`regen.rs:109-112`: `generated_content` clones every file's generated
string via `generated.to_owned()`, and `disk_content` clones every
file's disk bytes into a `String` via `String::from_utf8_lossy(...).
into_owned()`. Both run for *every* file in the project, before the
`match` that decides the entry's status — including for entries that
resolve to `RegenStatus::Unchanged`, which `apply_regeneration`
(`regen.rs:308`) explicitly does nothing with, and which no diff
renderer ever reads. `sim-three-service`/typescript is 806 KB of
generated output per build.

**Item 10's target has literally zero indexes.** `graph.rs:168-185`:
`SystemGraph` is a `String`, a `bool`, and seven bare `Vec`s. The four
hot accessors are all linear scans — `nodes_in_service` (`:325-329`),
`find_named_in_service` (`:353-361`, which filters
`nodes_in_service`, making it a scan over a scan), `edges_from` and
`edges_to` (`:363-369`), and `pipeline_of` (`:372-374`). They are
called from roughly 47 sites across `ciac-sema` and `ciac-codegen`,
most inside loops. `perf_scaling.rs` measured `sema::analyze` growth
at **1.93× / 2.80× / 2.72×** across N=100/200/400 — under that gate's
own 4.5× ceiling, but nothing like the flat curve indexing should
produce — and `31UpdatePlan.md` M8's callgrind breakdown of `ciac
check` at N=400 attributed **31.75%** of instructions to
`Vec::from_iter`, **12.28%** to `find_named_in_service`, **8.07%** to
`Reachability::run`, and **6.43%** to `wire_steps`, with `malloc`/
`free` together near 13%.

**Item 1's `OnceLock` does nothing for a CLI process, and the lazy
path is already available.** `template.rs:39-41` runs
`for (name, source) in templates { env.add_template(name, source)?; }`
inside `environment()`, parsing every one of a backend's embedded
templates eagerly. `cached_environment` (`:69-82`, added by
`30UpdatePlan.md` M4) wraps that in a per-backend `OnceLock`, which
amortizes the cost across *repeated* `generate()` calls within one
process — exactly what `determinism.rs`'s double-generate does, and
exactly what a `ciac build` invocation does *not* do, since it calls
`generate()` once and exits. Root `Cargo.toml:57` already pins
`minijinja = { version = "2", features = ["loader"] }`, so
`Environment::set_loader` is a supported swap on an already-present
feature rather than new machinery or a new dependency.

**Item 9's redundancy is three call sites, not a loop.**
`crates/ciac/src/commands.rs:2598`, `:2701` and `:3161` each call
`ciac_codegen::model::build_system(&ir, ...)` independently. These are
distinct command paths rather than three calls in one build, so the
dedupe available is narrower than "call it once instead of three
times" — M6 states the correct scope rather than inheriting the
inventory's phrasing.

### What this arc does not do

It adds no new gate. `31UpdatePlan.md` built three and promoted none
of them, for a reason it stated carefully: promotion requires a
20-merge soak that a single continuous session cannot honestly
manufacture. Adding a fourth gate while two remain unpromoted would be
gate inflation, and this arc's M9 tightens an existing gate instead.

It also does not optimize anything outside the twelve-item inventory,
however tempting a profile line looks mid-arc. The inventory was
produced by a systematic investigation and ranked before any of it was
implemented; opportunistically adding a thirteenth item mid-flight
would mean shipping one change whose threshold was chosen after seeing
its own result, which is precisely the discipline this arc's Pillar 1
exists to prevent.

## Pillar 1 — Every threshold is pre-registered, in this document, before its code exists

`31UpdatePlan.md` Pillar 1 derived every gate band from measured noise
*before* choosing it, on the reasoning that "a threshold chosen after
seeing the data is a threshold fitted to the data, and it will be
chosen — consciously or not — to make the current state pass." That
reasoning applies with equal force one level up, to the question of
whether an optimization *worked*.

The failure mode is specific and easy to fall into: implement a
change, measure a 4% gain, and decide 4% was always worth having.
Every optimization looks worth keeping once it exists and the diff is
already written; sunk cost does the rest. The counter is to write the
number down first.

So each milestone below states a threshold in this document, before
that milestone's implementation commit, and Contract 3's graduated
table — not discretion at milestone-close time — decides what happens
when the measurement lands. The thresholds are derived from the tables
above by explicit reasoning stated at each milestone, never picked for
roundness.

## Pillar 2 — Byte identity is the contract, and it is machine-checked

This arc's central risk is not that an optimization fails to help. It
is that an optimization helps *and* silently changes output. A hash
threaded from the wrong place, an index that disagrees with the scan it
replaced, a clone elided where the value was still read, a template
loader that resolves a name differently — each is a plausible bug in
this arc's specific changes, and each would produce wrong generated
code rather than a compile error.

Four independent layers already exist and are sufficient; this arc
adds none and weakens none:

1. **145 golden snapshots** across five backends. Byte-exact. `cargo
   insta` reports any pending snapshot as a failure.
2. **`determinism.rs`** generates every example twice and asserts the
   two runs are byte-identical — which catches nondeterminism a single
   snapshot comparison structurally cannot, and is directly relevant
   to M6, where a `HashMap`-backed index could introduce iteration
   order into a path that previously had none.
3. **`conformance.rs`, `openapi.rs`, `golden.rs`, `tests/ui/`** — the
   behavioral and negative corpora.
4. **`scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh`** — 50 program×target combinations that
   *run* the generated code. This is the strongest layer, because a
   change that alters program behavior without altering a snapshot's
   bytes is impossible by construction, but a change that alters bytes
   in a way that happens to still match a stale snapshot is not.

The exit checklist for every milestone runs all four. Layer 4 is the
one that costs minutes and the one most tempting to skip on a
"trivially safe" milestone; it is not skippable, and M6 in particular
is exactly the milestone where the temptation and the risk peak
together.

## Pillar 3 — The whole suite runs at every milestone boundary, not only at the arc's close

The user-facing requirement this arc was commissioned under is
explicit: run the whole benchmark suite at every milestone, check for
regressions and progressions, and roll back where necessary. That is
also the only way Contract 2 is enforceable — a trade discovered at M9
cannot be attributed to the milestone that made it.

The sequence runs **twice per milestone**: once immediately before the
milestone's first commit, confirming a clean starting point (a
milestone that begins from an already-broken tree cannot attribute its
own failure correctly), and once after its work lands.

```sh
# Correctness first. A fast milestone that changes output is a failure,
# and there is no point measuring a tree that is already wrong.
cargo test --workspace
git status --porcelain -- tests/tests/snapshots/     # must be empty
find . -name '*.snap.new' -not -path './target/*'    # must be empty
scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh                             # 50 combos, all PASS

# Then the measurement.
cargo build --release -p ciac
cargo run --release -p ciac-integration-tests --bin ciac-bench -- \
    --compare=docs/perf/baseline.json --with-callgrind --with-scaling
cargo test -p ciac-integration-tests --test perf_budget
cargo test -p ciac-integration-tests --test perf_baseline -- --ignored
cargo test --release -p ciac-integration-tests --test perf_scaling -- --ignored
scripts/bench-codegen.sh
scripts/bench-callgrind.sh --metric allocations
```

M8 additionally runs `--with-verify --with-slow-tests --with-sim`.
Those arms cost real minutes — 467.82 s, 210.32 s and 133.43 s
respectively at baseline — and they are M8's own subject rather than
overhead every other milestone should pay for nothing. Running them
everywhere would add roughly thirteen minutes per milestone boundary
to measure things no other milestone touches, which is the same
category of mistake as `31UpdatePlan.md`'s own second contract warned
against: an instrument that becomes the cost.

## Pillar 4 — Two baselines, so that no delta is ever ambiguous

A single rolling baseline answers "what did this milestone do" and
loses "what has the arc done." A single frozen baseline answers the
reverse. Keeping one file and hoping to remember which question it is
answering is how an arc loses track of which change bought which win.

So, two files, deliberately:

- **`docs/perf/baseline-v0.29.0.json`** — frozen at M1, never
  rewritten for the remainder of this arc. Every milestone's
  *cumulative* delta is measured against it, and M9's retrospective
  table is built entirely from it.
- **`docs/perf/baseline.json`** — the existing file, updated after each
  *kept* milestone (never after a reverted one), so that the next
  milestone's `--compare` output shows that milestone's own
  *incremental* delta rather than the whole arc's accumulated one.

Every `--update-baseline` carries its justification in the commit
message, continuing the discipline `docs/perf/README.md` already
states and `31UpdatePlan.md` M4 established. The frozen file is
committed once, at M1, with a commit message stating plainly that it
is immutable for the arc's duration — so that a later reader finding
it unchanged knows that was intent rather than neglect.

## Pillar 5 — Rollback is graduated, and the half-threshold rule is the interesting part

Reverting on a correctness failure needs no argument. The rule that
does is the one about a change that *works*, just not enough.

An optimization that lands 3% against a 10% threshold has not failed
in any obvious way. It made things faster. Reverting it feels like
throwing away free performance. But it also added a code path, a
lifetime, an invariant somebody must maintain, and a paragraph of
explanation in a file someone will read in two years — and it bought a
number smaller than this arc's own measurement noise on some of the
metrics involved. Complexity is a real, permanent cost paid against a
gain that was, by the arc's own pre-registered judgment, not the point.

Hence the graduated table, in full:

| Condition | Action |
|---|---|
| Any golden snapshot moves | **Revert.** Contract 1 breach. |
| `determinism.rs` fails | **Revert.** Contract 1 breach — and specifically the nondeterminism M6 could plausibly introduce. |
| `scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh` fails | **Revert.** Contract 1 breach, at the behavioral layer. |
| `perf_budget.rs` fails | **Revert.** The one currently-blocking gate. |
| `perf_baseline.rs` reports >1% instruction growth | **Revert.** |
| `perf_scaling.rs` exceeds its 4.5× / 3.0× ceilings | **Revert.** |
| Any unrelated metric regresses beyond its noise band | **Revert**, unless the trade is stated and accepted in the milestone report. |
| Gain < ½ the pre-registered threshold | **Revert.** The complexity is not paid for. |
| Gain ≥ ½ but < the threshold | **Keep**, and record the shortfall explicitly. |
| Gain ≥ threshold | **Keep.** |

The mechanics: each milestone is one commit where possible (M3 and M6
may be two — implementation, then the test corpus proving it). A
rollback is `git revert` of that milestone's commits, pushed, with the
outcome recorded in this document's running log rather than silently
dropped. A reverted milestone leaves behind a measured negative
result, which is a genuine contribution to the next arc's inventory —
"we tried indexing and the ratio did not move" is worth more than an
absent row.

## Pillar 6 — Fix the data structure, not the symptom

Ten of the twelve items are local changes: hash once, clone less, load
lazily, skip a no-op, share a target directory. Item 10 is not. It is
the only item in the inventory that changes a *representation*, and it
is the only one whose absence will keep producing new performance
problems indefinitely.

`SystemGraph` answers "which node in this service has this name" by
scanning every node in the graph, filtering by service, then filtering
by kind and name. Every consumer that needs that answer inside a loop
turns it into a quadratic. The 31.75% of `ciac check` spent in
`Vec::from_iter` is not one bad call site — it is the accumulated cost
of an interface that makes the linear scan the only thing on offer.

The fix is to add indexes behind the existing accessors without
changing a single public signature, so that all ~47 call sites remain
untouched and the change is provable by construction rather than by
review of every caller. That is also what makes it safe to attempt at
all: the diff is confined to one file, and the correctness question
reduces to "does the index agree with the scan it replaced, at every
point where the graph can mutate."

That question is answered by exhaustive audit, not by testing —
`add_node_owned` (`graph.rs:199-213`) and `add_edge` (`:236-247`,
whose existing dedupe-on-insert semantics must be preserved exactly)
are the two obvious paths, but `pipelines` is a `pub` field
(`graph.rs:182`) pushed to directly from `ciac-sema`'s builder, which
means the compiler will not tell us if a mutation site is missed. M6's
first task is enumerating those sites, before a line of index code is
written.

## Pillar 7 — The checkpoint has real teeth, including the authority to stop

M5 is a hard serialization point: no commit under M6, M7 or M8 lands
before M5's own commit does. This mirrors `31UpdatePlan.md` M5, which
gated its own M6–M9 the same way and for the same reason — the
decisions M5 makes are cheap before the downstream work exists and
expensive after.

Its four pre-registered outcomes are stated at the milestone. The one
worth flagging here is (d): **the arc stops at M5 and ships M1–M4 as
the release.** That is written down as a legitimate success, not a
fallback, because `30UpdatePlan.md` M5 did exactly this — stopped an
arc early on the grounds that its early milestones had already beaten
the target — and was right to. A checkpoint that cannot stop the work
is a status meeting.

## Determinism and supply chain

**No new dependencies.** Not one crate is added to `Cargo.toml` and no
new entry appears in `Cargo.lock` except the version bump's own
regeneration at M9. `minijinja`'s `loader` feature (M4) is already
enabled at `Cargo.toml:57`. `valgrind` is already a CI dependency for
the `perf-gate` job, added by `31UpdatePlan.md` M6. `deny.toml` is
untouched, and `cargo-audit`/`cargo-deny` gain no new surface to
police.

The one milestone that could plausibly want a dependency is M8's
parallel validation, where `rayon` is the obvious reach. It is not
taken: `std::thread::scope` over a bounded set of independent projects
is sufficient for a workload whose parallelism is measured in single
digits, and adding a thread-pool dependency to a workspace that has
been deliberate about its tree — in order to parallelize five
subprocess invocations — is not a trade this arc makes.

**Determinism of the compiler is at genuine risk in exactly one
milestone, and it is M6.** The other eleven items either cannot affect
iteration order (hashing, cloning, early-out, template parsing) or do
not touch the compiler at all (items 5–8). M6 introduces hash-based
lookups into a structure whose entire iteration contract is insertion
order — `graph.rs:163-167` says so explicitly: "Node and edge iteration
follows insertion order, which the builder in `ciac-sema` derives from
declaration order — making every downstream consumer (passes, dumps,
codegen) deterministic by construction."

The mitigation is structural rather than hopeful: indexes are used
only for *lookup by key*, never for iteration. `nodes()`, `edges()`,
`nodes_of_kind()` and `nodes_in_service()` keep iterating the `Vec`s in
insertion order and are not backed by any index. An index maps a key
to an ID or a `Vec<EdgeId>` whose own order is insertion order. If a
milestone-M6 change makes any *iteration* traverse a `HashMap`,
`determinism.rs` will catch it — and the design rule above means it
should never get that far.

3. **M3 — The `source_hash` early-out.** The single largest multiplier

   Implement, in `cmd_build` before generation: if a manifest loads,
   **and** its `source_hash` equals the freshly computed source hash,
   **and** its `recipe` is `Some` and equal field-by-field to the
   current invocation's recipe, **and** every path in `manifest.files`
   exists on disk with a matching hash — print an informational "up to

   is nearly as expensive as the work it avoids, which would be an
   argument against the feature rather than a disappointing result.

   second commit within this milestone: the skip fires on an untouched

## Open questions resolved at implementation (pre-registered)

Recorded now, with their decision rules, so the answers cannot be
reverse-engineered from whatever turns out to be convenient.

1. **Does `--force` belong on `build`, or is `--incremental` opt-in the
   safer shape?** Resolved in advance: **default-on with `--force`**.
   An opt-in flag that nobody passes delivers no value to the
   developer running `ciac build` in a loop, which is the entire
   population the optimization exists for. The safety argument is
   answered by Pillar 5's fail-closed design and M3's mandatory test
   corpus rather than by hiding the feature. **Reversal rule:** if
   M3's own tests, or any downstream milestone's suite run, produce a
   single case where the early-out skips a build that should have run,
   the feature converts to `--incremental` opt-in in the same
   milestone, and that conversion is recorded as a deviation rather
   than a design choice.

2. **How much of `build_manifest`'s 362.4 µs is hashing?** Unknown
   before M2 and deliberately not guessed. If the callgrind delta after
   threading hashes through comes in under M2's 8% threshold, the
   answer is "less than assumed," and Contract 3's graduated table
   decides — under 4%, revert; between 4% and 8%, keep and record. The
   temptation this pre-registration defends against is re-deriving the
   threshold downward once the measurement is in hand.

3. **Can `RegenEntry` carry borrowed content instead of owned?** M7's
   preferred shape is deferring the `String` materialization into the
   `match` arms, not introducing lifetimes onto `RegenEntry`. The
   struct is `pub` and `Clone`, crosses into `commands.rs`, and is held
   past the borrow of the `GeneratedProject` in at least one caller.
   **Rule:** if a lifetime is required to hit M7's 20% threshold, the
   threshold loses — a public struct gaining a lifetime parameter to
   buy allocation count is the wrong trade in a compiler whose regen
   plan is already the subtlest code in `ciac-codegen`.

4. **Which `validate` steps are order-independent?** M8 must answer per
   target rather than assuming. The rule: a step is parallelizable with
   its siblings only if it neither writes into the project tree nor
   reads output another step writes. `gofmt` (writes) and `go build`
   (produces artifacts `go test` consumes) are serial with their
   neighbours; `ruff` and `pytest` are independent; `tsc`, `eslint` and
   `vitest` need checking against whether `tsc` emits. **Default when
   uncertain: serial.** A wrong parallelization here produces a flaky
   validation failure, which is worse than a slow one.

5. **Does `set_loader` change minijinja's template-resolution
   semantics for `include`/`extends`?** M4 must confirm that templates
   referencing each other by name resolve identically through the
   loader as through `add_template`. **Rule:** the golden corpus is the
   arbiter — every backend's full template graph is exercised by it. If
   any snapshot moves, M4 reverts regardless of its measured setup
   gain, per Contract 1, with no discussion of whether the new
   resolution is "also correct."

6. **Should the frozen baseline live in `docs/perf/` or outside the
   docs tree?** Resolved: `docs/perf/baseline-v0.29.0.json`, beside the
   rolling file. It is data a future reader auditing this arc will want
   next to the thing it is compared against, and `docs/perf/README.md`'s
   instrument index is already the place someone looks.

## Verification strategy

- **Pillar 3's full suite at every milestone boundary**, before and
  after, with results recorded per the exit checklist. This is the
  arc's primary verification mechanism and its most expensive
  commitment; it is also the thing the arc was commissioned to do.
- **`scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh` at every milestone**, without exception

- **`determinism.rs` treated as M6's specific gate**, not merely part
  of the suite. It is the test that would catch a `HashMap` leaking
  into an iteration path, which is M6's characteristic failure mode.
- **CI polled to a terminal state after every push** via the GitHub

- **M9's cumulative accounting** against `baseline-v0.29.0.json`:

## Milestone dependencies and parallelism

```
M1 ──▶ M2 ──▶ M3 ──┐
       └──▶ M4 ─────┼──▶ M5 (CHECKPOINT) ──┬──▶ M6 ──┐
                    │                       ├──▶ M7 ──┼──▶ M9
                    │                       └──▶ M8 ──┘
```

**M2 must precede M3, and the ordering is not negotiable.** Once M3's
early-out skips the regeneration path on a warm build, M2's benefit on
that path becomes unmeasurable — the code no longer runs in the
scenario where it was fastest. M2's number has to be taken while the
warm path still executes.

**M4 is independent of both** and could run concurrently with either;
it touches only `template.rs` and its metric (`template_setup`) is not
affected by the regen path at all.

**M5 is a hard serialization point.** M6, M7 and M8 are mutually
independent — different files, different metrics, no shared state —
and could in principle proceed in parallel, but each still takes its
own full suite run at its own boundary, since Contract 2 is only
enforceable if a regression can be attributed to one milestone.

## Explicit cuts

- **No new gates.** `31UpdatePlan.md` built three and promoted none.
  Adding a fourth while two remain unpromoted would be gate inflation
  that the promotion checklist exists to discourage. M9 tightens an
  existing gate instead.
- **No SHA-NI-specific work.** The 39.79% hashing figure was measured
  on a machine without SHA-NI, and `sha2` already dispatches to
  hardware acceleration at runtime where it exists. Hashing *less*
  (M2) helps on every machine; hand-tuning the hash helps on one and is
  out of scope.
- **No parallel code generation.** `generate()` is 0.5–1.3 ms for four
  of five backends. Threading it would add failure modes to a
  short-lived CLI process to buy microseconds against a `write_to` that
  costs more.
- **No incremental compilation of `.ciac` sources.** The front end is
  146 µs on `order-system` (`syntax::load` 56.1 + `sema::analyze`
  90.0). M6 fixes the growth curve of the part that scales badly;
  nothing in the measured data justifies a dependency-tracked
  incremental front end.
- **No `rayon`.** See "Determinism and supply chain."
- **No persistent formatter daemon.** See Pillar 6 and M7.

## Risks

- **M6 can break things silently, and it is the only milestone that
  can.** A stale index after an unaudited mutation path produces a
  *wrong lookup*, not a crash — the worst failure shape available in a
  compiler, because it ships. Mitigation is ordered: enumerate every
  mutation site *first*; build indexes incrementally in the same
  commit; use indexes for lookup only, never iteration;
  `determinism.rs` and `sim-corpus-x5.sh` as the backstop. If the
  enumeration turns up mutation sites outside `graph.rs` that cannot
  be routed through a maintained method, M6 narrows to the accessors
  that *can* be — partial indexing is an acceptable outcome; unaudited
  indexing is not.
- **M3 changes default CLI behavior.** A too-eager skip means a
  developer's real edit does not rebuild, which is the single worst
  thing a compiler can do. Mitigation: fail-closed on every condition
  (Pillar 5), a mandatory test corpus, `--force` as an always-available
  escape, and open question 1's pre-registered reversal rule.
- **Thresholds were set from data but before the code existed.** Some
  will prove optimistic. Contract 3's graduated table is the
  mitigation, and M5 is the designated place to admit it early rather
  than discovering it at M9 with three more milestones already built on
  the assumption.
- **This machine has 4 vCPUs and no SHA-NI.** M8's parallelism numbers
  in particular will read differently on a 16-core runner, and M2's
  hashing gain will read *smaller* on a SHA-NI machine where the hash
  is cheaper to begin with. Ratios travel; absolutes do not. Every
  threshold in this document is a ratio for that reason.
- **The arc is long and the suite is expensive.** Nine milestones × two
  full suite runs is the dominant time cost, and the temptation to skip
  the "before" run on a milestone that follows immediately from a green
  "after" is real. It is also mostly safe — which is why the rule is
  stated as absolute rather than left to judgment, since a rule with a
  reasonable exception erodes to no rule.

## Confidence and handoff

This document is written whole, before M1's own commit, per the
standing precedent that a plan is authored and then executed milestone
by milestone rather than discovered as it goes. Every threshold above
was chosen before the code that must clear it exists; the running log
and M9's retrospective are where this document is held accountable for
which of those numbers survived contact with reality.

The handoff to the *next* arc is this: after M9, each of the twelve
inventory items has not merely an instrument but a measured result —
kept with a number, or reverted with a number and a reason. An
inventory row that reads "reverted: indexes landed, growth ratio
unchanged at 2.7×" is worth strictly more to whoever picks this up
than a row that reads "pending," because it closes a hypothesis
instead of deferring it.
