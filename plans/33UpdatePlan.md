# CIaC v0.33-file — The Wrong Axis: Re-cutting the Slow Test Binaries Where the Time Actually Is (implementation plan)

> Implementation plan. Document number ≠ release number (standing
> precedent; expected to ship as **0.31.0**). Assumes
> `26UpdatePlan.md`–`32UpdatePlan.md` shipped, the last of them as
> 0.30.0.
>
> This arc is narrower than its predecessor by design. `32UpdatePlan.md`
> implemented twelve optimizations across nine milestones. This one
> re-opens exactly **two rows of that inventory** — the two whose
> results were, respectively, a disclosed shortfall and an outright cut
> — and finishes them properly:
>
> | # | Inventory item | v0.32 outcome | This arc |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 7 | Split `determinism.rs` for libtest | **Kept, shortfall disclosed** — 4.46% against a ≥30% threshold | **M3–M5**, re-cut on the axis that pays |
> | 11 | Warm/eliminate the Java formatter | **Cut** at M7 | **M6**, revived in its narrow, measured form |
>
> The reason to re-open item 7 is not that 4.46% was disappointing. It
> is that **the explanation recorded for it is wrong**, and a wrong
> explanation in a committed plan is more expensive than no explanation
> — it tells the next reader the problem is the hardware, and the next
> reader believes it, because it was written down next to a real
> measurement. `32UpdatePlan.md`'s running log attributes the shortfall
> to this machine's core count. This document shows, by measurement,
> that the shortfall is **Amdahl's law on a badly chosen split axis**,
> that it would reproduce identically on a 64-core machine, and that
> the same work re-cut along a different axis clears the original
> threshold with room to spare.
>
> **Contract A — byte identity.** Every generated byte, on every
> target, for every example, is identical before and after this arc.
> The claim is easier to make here than in v0.32 and should not be
> mistaken for a weaker one: that arc touched `ciac-codegen`,
> `ciac-ir` and the CLI and still promised the output could not tell.
> This arc touches **test harnesses** and **one JVM startup flag**.
> Neither lever touches a template, a context struct, or any codegen
> decision — #1 changes only *when* independent `generate()` calls
> happen relative to each other, and #2 changes only *how the JVM
> loads its own classes*. The enforcement mechanism is unchanged and
> is not modified by this arc: the 145 golden snapshots
> (`tests/tests/snapshots/golden__gen__{python,rust,typescript,go,java}__*.snap`
> — 29 examples × 5 backends, every example supporting every backend),
> `determinism.rs`, `conformance.rs`, `openapi.rs`, `golden.rs`, the
> `tests/ui/` negative corpus, and `scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh` — 50
> program×target combinations that *execute* generated code rather
> than merely diffing it. Any snapshot movement at all is a bug, and
> there is no disclosed exception anywhere in this arc.
>
> **Contract B — the test surface is preserved exactly, and this is
> the contract this arc can most easily breach.** Every assertion that
> runs today still runs after this arc, on the same inputs, the same
> number of times. No test is skipped, merged, weakened, sampled, or
> memoized. The specific temptation, named now so it cannot be
> rationalized later: `determinism.rs` generates every example
> **twice** and compares the two runs, and roughly half this arc's
> headline number could be obtained in an afternoon by memoizing
> `generate()` so the second call is free. **That double generation is
> the test.** A change that makes the second generation cheap by not
> performing it has not optimized the suite; it has deleted a test and
> kept its name. Any diff in this arc that reduces assertion count,
> input count, or invocation count is a defect, not a win.
>
> **Contract C — no metric may regress to buy another.** Inherited
> verbatim in spirit from `32UpdatePlan.md` Contract 2. A milestone
> that improves its own named metric while pushing another outside its
> noise band is a *trade*, not a win, and the trade must be stated and
> accepted explicitly at that milestone rather than discovered three
> milestones later. `ciac-bench --compare` reports every metric it
> knows about, not only the one a milestone targets, precisely so a
> silent trade cannot hide in the part of the report nobody was
> reading.
>
> **Contract D — new to this arc: concurrency is pinned by a test
> before it is introduced.** Every other contract here is about not
> changing behavior. This one is about not introducing a class of bug
> the suite cannot currently catch. Three of six milestones add
> multi-threading to code that has never run multi-threaded, and the
> characteristic failure of that work is *intermittent*, not
> deterministic — a single green run proves close to nothing. M2 lands
> the concurrency-safety test and the atomicity fix **before** M3
> introduces the first thread, and the exit checklist requires repeat
> runs rather than one pass. See Pillar 8.
>
> **Confidence:** high on M3 and M4 — the mechanism is measured
> (2.26×), the code is test-harness-only, the blast radius stops at
> the test binary, and the primitive is already used twice in this
> workspace. High on M6's *mechanism* and moderate on its *ergonomics*
> — the 34% reduction and the byte-identity of its output are both
> directly measured, but AppCDS ships default-on, which means a cold
> machine pays a one-time archive-minting cost, and that cost is
> pre-registered as a threshold rather than waved past. Moderate on
> M5: `insta` carries thread-local state, so `golden.rs` needs a
> different shape from its three siblings, and the shape chosen trades
> some of the available win for keeping snapshot semantics exactly as
> they are. Every number quoted below was measured during this
> document's own preparation, on this machine — **ratios are expected
> to travel to other hardware; absolute timings are not**, and no
> threshold in this document is stated as an absolute for that reason.
> That caveat is doing real work here, and it cuts *against* the
> previous arc's conclusion rather than for it: see the next section.

## The gap this version closes

`32UpdatePlan.md` M8 item 7 split `determinism.rs` and `openapi.rs`
from one `#[test]` fn each into five apiece — one per backend — so
that libtest would have something to parallelize. It measured a
**4.46%** reduction against a **≥30%** threshold. That is under half
the threshold, which is Contract 3's literal revert condition in that
document's own graduated table; it was kept anyway, with the shortfall
disclosed, on the strength of the following root cause, quoted here in
full from that document's running log:

> Root cause, checked rather than assumed: `determinism.rs`'s own
> split (58.02s → 55.81s, only 3.8%) shows libtest *did* schedule the
> 5 per-backend functions concurrently, but java's own `generate()`
> call spawns the vendored `google-java-format` JVM (with its own
> internal thread pool for JIT/GC), and this machine has only 4 vCPUs
> — java's single logical test thread already saturates the available
> cores by itself, leaving no spare capacity for the other 4 (much
> cheaper) backends to make concurrent progress on.

The measurement is right. The diagnosis is wrong, in a way that
matters, and both halves of it are wrong independently.

### First: java does not saturate the box

The claim is directly testable — run N formatter invocations serially,
then the same N concurrently, and see whether the wall time moves. It
moves:

| Formatter invocations | Serial | Parallel | Speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 2.55s | 1.13s | **2.26×** |
| 8 (on 4 vCPUs) | — | — | held ~2.3× |

If one java formatter run saturated four cores, four of them
concurrently would take the same wall time as four serially, and the
speedup column would read 1.0×. It reads 2.26×. The JVM's internal
JIT/GC threads are real, but they are not what the wall clock is
spent on — a formatter run is dominated by *classloading*, which is
substantially serial and I/O-and-branch-bound within a single JVM, and
therefore leaves most of the machine idle. Four idle-ish JVMs overlap
almost as well as four independent processes normally do.

### Second, and more important: the split was on the wrong axis

Here is where `determinism.rs`'s time actually goes, measured
per-backend with each test run in isolation:

| Backend | Time | Share of total |
|---|---|---|
| python | 0.35s | 0.6% |
| rust | 0.27s | 0.5% |
| typescript | 0.32s | 0.6% |
| go | 1.62s | 2.9% |
| **java** | **52.82s** | **95.3%** |
| *(other four combined)* | *(2.56s)* | *(4.7%)* |

Java is **twenty times** the other four backends put together.

Now apply Amdahl's law to the split that was actually made. Splitting a
55.4s serial workload into five *independent* test functions, and
running those five perfectly in parallel on infinite cores, yields a
wall time of `max(52.82, 0.35, 0.27, 0.32, 1.62)` = **52.82s**. The
best achievable reduction is therefore `2.56 / 55.38` = **4.6%**.

The measured result was **4.46%**.

The split worked. libtest scheduled the functions concurrently exactly
as intended, and the implementation captured **97% of the theoretical
maximum available to it**. The optimization was not hardware-starved;
it was *structurally capped at 4.6% before a line of it was written*,
and it very nearly hit that cap. Buying a 64-core machine would have
produced 4.6%. Buying a 1024-core machine would have produced 4.6%.

**This is the correction this document exists to record.** The
threshold was missed not because the machine was too small but because
the work was divided along an axis on which 95% of it sits in a single
piece. The v0.32 note invokes that arc's own "ratios travel; absolutes
do not" caveat in its defense, and the invocation is misapplied: the
4.46% *is* a ratio, and it travels perfectly. It will reproduce, within
noise, on every machine anyone ever runs it on.

### Where the time really is, and how much of it is recoverable

The formatter's cost is almost entirely fixed per *invocation*, not
per *file*:

| Formatter invocation | Wall time |
|---|---|
| 1 file | 0.64s |
| 40 files | 0.92s |

Solving the two-point line: **fixed ≈ 0.63s, marginal ≈ 7ms/file.**
The fixed part is roughly **90× the marginal part**. This is the same
shape `30UpdatePlan.md` M2 already exploited once — that arc collapsed
one JVM spawn *per file* into one spawn per `generate()`, and the
existing comment at `crates/ciac-backend-java/src/lib.rs:1004-1012`
still records that it was worth ~20s on a single 40-file build. What
remains after that fix is one full JVM startup plus one javac-internals
classload **per `generate()` call**.

The four slow binaries make a lot of those calls. Every one of the 29
examples currently supports all five backends — confirmed by counting
`golden__gen__*` snapshots, which come to 145 split exactly 29 apiece
across the five — so the count below is exact rather than estimated.
(The corpus is gated per-backend via `check_support`, so this is a
property of today's examples rather than a structural guarantee; an
example added later that gates out a backend would shift these
numbers, which is worth knowing when re-measuring against this table
in a future arc.)

| Binary | java `generate()` calls | Derivation |
|---|---|---|
| `determinism` | 58 | 29 examples × **2** (the double-generate *is* the test) |
| `conformance` | 87 | 3 test fns × 29 examples, each generating all backends |
| `golden` | 29 | 29 examples × 1 |
| `openapi` | 29 | 29 examples × 1 |
| **total** | **203** | |

At ~0.63s of fixed cost apiece, that is **~128 seconds of repeated JVM
startup and classloading that performs no incremental work** — against
a measured slow-binary total of **167.35s** warm (185.50s as
`baseline.json` records it). Roughly **three-quarters of the entire
slow-binary budget is one JVM starting up, over and over** — 76%
against the warm total, 69% against the committed one. The conclusion
does not depend on which denominator M1 settles on, which is the point
of stating both.

Two independent levers act on that 128s, and they multiply:

1. **It parallelizes** (2.26× measured). Attacking it *across the 29
   examples* divides the 95% instead of the 4.7%. This is item 7,
   re-cut.
2. **It shrinks** (34% measured). An AppCDS class-data-sharing archive
   pre-digests the classloading that dominates each startup, taking
   the fixed cost from **0.638s → 0.420s**, with output verified
   byte-identical. This is item 11, revived.

Applied together to `determinism.rs`'s java test:
`52.8 ÷ 2.26 × (0.69 / 0.91) ≈ 17s`, against 52.82s today.

**Target for the arc: ~167s → ~87s at thresholds, ~61s at measured
ceilings (−48% to −64%)**, either of which clears the ≥30% bar item 7
originally set and missed. The denominator here is the single warm run
taken during this document's preparation, before M1 executed. **M1 has
since run** — three independent 3-rep-median readings taken in
isolation, nothing else running, per Pillar 4 — **and settled the true
figure lower still: 135.30s** (determinism 46.86s, conformance 42.87s,
golden 23.10s, openapi 22.47s; full detail and the fourth corroborating
data point in the running log's M1 row). That is the number every
milestone's own before/after is actually gated against, per Pillar 3 —
each milestone takes a fresh isolated reading at its own boundary
rather than trusting a document-wide constant, so this reconciliation
does not propagate error into M3–M6's individual verdicts, only into
how the arc-level prose target below should be read.

That headline is an arc-level number, and an arc-level number is the
easiest kind to arrive at by accident. Broken down per binary, using
each milestone's own pre-registered threshold rather than the measured
ceilings, it is auditable — built during document preparation against
the single-run figures above, and **retained here for the shape of the
derivation rather than re-derived against M1's lower total**, since the
worked arithmetic underneath it (java's 58 calls × ~0.63s fixed cost)
was itself measured against an isolated java-only reading (52.82s) that
sits between the two full-binary totals. Direction and rough proportion
travel; the absolute "Implied after" column should be read as
illustrative, with each milestone's own fresh measurement as the
authority:

| Binary | Before (single-run) | Milestone | Threshold applied | Implied after | Delta |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `determinism` | 54.74s | M6 then M3 | −25% of fixed, then ≥1.8× | ~26s | −52% |
| `conformance` | 50.94s | M6 then M4 | −25% of fixed, then ≥1.6× | ~27s | −47% |
| `golden` | 31.93s | M6 then M5 | −25% of fixed, then ≥1.5× | ~18s | −44% |
| `openapi` | 29.74s | M6 then M4 | −25% of fixed, then ≥1.6× | ~16s | −46% |
| **total** | **167.35s** | | | **~87s** | **−48%** |

Applying the same threshold ratios to M1's lower, more rigorous
135.30s total instead — the more honest arc-level number now that it
exists — projects **~74s at thresholds, ~52s at measured ceilings**,
still comfortably clearing ≥30% either way.

Worked example for the top row, so the arithmetic is checkable rather
than asserted: `determinism` makes 58 java `generate()` calls, each
carrying ~0.63s of fixed JVM cost = 36.5s of the java test's 52.82s.
M6 cuts that fixed component by ≥25%, removing ~9.1s and leaving java
at ~43.7s. M3 then parallelizes what remains by ≥1.8×, giving ~24.3s,
and the four cheap backends continue to overlap inside that window
rather than adding to it — landing the binary near 26s.

Three things about this table are worth stating explicitly, because all
three cut against the headline rather than for it.

First, **the order of operations matters and is not commutative in
presentation.** M6 is applied before M3 above because AppCDS shrinks a
per-call cost while parallelism divides a total; applying them in the
other order yields the same product but invites double-counting the
same seconds. Whichever milestone lands first in practice, M7 measures
them cumulatively against one frozen baseline, which is the only
formulation immune to the error.

Second, **the table projects ~87s while the measured ceilings suggest
~61s**, and the difference is not an error in either. The ceilings come
from what was actually measured (2.26× parallel, 34% AppCDS); the ~87s
comes from the *pre-registered thresholds*, deliberately set below those
ceilings so a milestone that underperforms slightly still clears its
bar. The honest statement of the arc's target is therefore a range, and
quoting only the optimistic end would be the same species of mistake
this arc exists to correct.

Third, **M6's contribution is smaller in this table than its 34%
suggests**, and that is arithmetic rather than pessimism. AppCDS
reduces the JVM's *fixed startup cost*, which is ~69% of the
slow-binary budget — so a 34% cut to that component is a ~23% cut
overall, and less again once M3–M5 have already overlapped much of it
with itself. The two levers multiply, but they multiply against
overlapping shares of the same total, and the second one to land always
looks weaker than it is in isolation. M7's checkpoint measures them
cumulatively for exactly this reason, and Pillar 7 outcome (c) exists
because it is genuinely possible that M3 and M4 alone make M5 and M6
not worth their complexity.

### What was verified in source before this plan was written

`32UpdatePlan.md` established the practice of checking each headline
claim against the actual code before choosing any threshold, on the
grounds that a plan built on a profile nobody re-read is the failure
mode the whole discipline exists to prevent. Every item below was read
this session, and each is cited so the implementer does not have to
re-audit it.

**`format_batch`'s scratch directories are already concurrency-safe,
and this is the single biggest reason lever #1 is cheap.** The obvious
fear about running `generate()` on multiple threads is that the Java
and Go backends write real files into a scratch directory and could
collide. They cannot.
`crates/ciac-codegen/src/format_batch.rs:73`'s `scratch_dir()` builds
its path from a `static AtomicU64` counter *and* `std::process::id()`,
and the doc comment immediately above it (`:69-72`) already states the
guarantee in as many words: "safe under concurrent `generate()` calls
from multiple threads in the same process (the test suite does exactly
that) and across processes (the pid component)." **No change is needed
here.** M2 records that finding in its commit message specifically so
the next person does not re-derive it.

**All five backends are zero-sized unit structs.** `PythonBackend`,
`RustBackend`, `TsBackend`, `GoBackend` and `JavaBackend` carry no
fields. Constructing one per worker thread costs nothing at runtime,
which is what makes the design in the next bullet affordable.

**`pub trait Backend` has no `Sync` or `Send` bound**
(`crates/ciac-codegen/src/lib.rs:142`). This is the constraint that
shapes M3's whole design. Sharing a single `&dyn Backend` across
worker threads would require adding `Sync` to the trait — a
**public-API change**, affecting every out-of-tree implementor and
contradicting `docs/external-backends.md`'s published contract, taken
on solely to speed up an internal test harness. That trade is not
made. Instead the helpers take a **backend factory** (`fn() -> B`) and
each thread constructs its own. The trait is untouched.

**`GeneratedProject` is fully owned**
(`crates/ciac-codegen/src/project.rs:34`): a `BTreeMap<String,
GeneratedFile>` plus a `Vec<String>`. It contains no borrows and is
therefore auto-`Send`, so generated output moves out of a worker
thread without any wrapper.

**`std::thread::scope` is the established house primitive, and the
precedent includes the reasoning.**
`crates/ciac/src/commands.rs:3652` and `:3718` both use it (added by
`32UpdatePlan.md` M8 item 5, the milestone's one unambiguous success
at 61.9–70.5%), and the doc comment at `:3626` explicitly records the
choice as "`std::thread::scope` — not `rayon`, per 'Determinism and
supply chain'." This arc follows that precedent rather than re-arguing
it, and adds no dependency.

**`vendored_jar_path()` performs a non-atomic check-then-write of a
3.6 MB jar** (`crates/ciac-backend-java/src/lib.rs:992`). It stats a
fixed path in `temp_dir()`, compares the length against the embedded
jar's, and calls `std::fs::write` if they differ. Two processes or
threads racing here can have one reading a partially written jar while
the other writes it. This is *already* reachable today —
`conformance.rs`'s three test fns run concurrently under libtest — but
it is rare enough not to have been observed. Lever #1 makes it far
hotter, which is why M2 fixes it **before** M3 exists rather than
after a flake appears.

**`-XX:SharedArchiveFile` is fail-soft, verified rather than assumed.**
A corrupt archive produces exit code 0 and a `[warning][cds]` line on
stderr; a missing archive produces exit code 0 silently. Neither
aborts the JVM, so **M6 needs no fallback branch** — the flag can be
passed unconditionally. Additionally, `format_batch_in` only reads
stderr when `!output.status.success()`, so the warning line cannot
leak into a user-visible error message on the success path.

**The existing JVM flag block is the right place to extend, and it
already documents a negative result.**
`crates/ciac-backend-java/src/lib.rs:1032-1051` explains
`-XX:TieredStopAtLevel=1` with its measurement (2142ms → 1413ms on
`sim-three-service`'s 55 files), records that `-XX:+UseSerialGC` was
tried alongside and was **slower** (1559ms) and must not be re-added
without a measurement, and states that these are startup-shape flags
which "cannot change a byte google-java-format emits, which the golden
snapshots enforce." M6's comment follows that template exactly,
including the measurement and the byte-identity argument.

**`tests/src/bench.rs:1373`'s doc comment is stale.** It reads: "The
fixed corpus: `determinism` and `openapi` each hold exactly one
`#[test]` fn (nothing for libtest to parallelize within either);
`conformance` and `golden` hold three apiece." `32UpdatePlan.md` M8
made it five apiece for the first two, and this arc changes what
parallelism means for all four. It is corrected at M8.

**CI's `Test` job has a 15-minute timeout**
(`.github/workflows/ci.yml:43-59`) and runs `cargo test --workspace`.
Everything in this arc moves that number down, so the timeout is
mentioned only to note it is not at risk.

### What this arc does not do

It does not touch the compiler. Not one line under `crates/` changes
except `ciac-backend-java`'s formatter invocation (M6) and its jar
materialization (M2), and neither can alter a generated byte. This is
a narrower footprint than any arc since `31UpdatePlan.md`, and it is
narrow on purpose: the work is worth doing and is not worth entangling
with a codegen change that would make a golden-snapshot movement
ambiguous.

It does not add a gate. `31UpdatePlan.md` built three and promoted
none; `32UpdatePlan.md` declined to add a fourth and tightened an
existing one instead. That reasoning is unchanged, and this arc has no
metric that wants a new gate anyway — the slow-binary total is already
tracked by `ciac-bench --with-slow-tests`.

It does not chase the third lever. See "Explicit cuts" for
`conformance.rs`'s 3× redundant generation, which is real, measured,
and deliberately left for the next arc with its reasoning recorded.

## Pillar 1 — Every threshold is pre-registered, in this document, before its code exists

Inherited unchanged from `32UpdatePlan.md` Pillar 1, whose statement of
the failure mode is exactly right and is not improved by rewording: "a
threshold chosen after seeing the data is a threshold fitted to the
data, and it will be chosen — consciously or not — to make the current
state pass."

This arc has a sharper reason to hold the line than its predecessor
did. It exists *because* a threshold was missed and the miss was
explained away. The explanation was offered in good faith and was
recorded openly rather than hidden — that part of v0.32's discipline
worked exactly as designed. But it was an explanation reached *after*
the number came in, reasoning from an unmeasured premise about the
hardware, and it was wrong. The defense against that is not more
integrity; v0.32 had plenty. The defense is to write down, in advance,
what the number has to be and what happens if it isn't — and then,
when a milestone misses, to **check the proposed explanation against a
measurement before recording it**, rather than accepting the first
plausible story.

So this arc adds one clause to Pillar 1: **a root cause offered for a
missed threshold is itself a claim, and it gets a measurement or it
gets marked as a hypothesis.** M8's note would have failed that test —
the "java saturates 4 vCPUs" premise was never run, and running it
takes about ninety seconds.

## Pillar 2 — Byte identity is the contract, and it is machine-checked

Four independent layers already exist; this arc adds none and weakens
none.

1. **145 golden snapshots** across five backends, byte-exact, with
   `cargo insta` reporting any pending snapshot as a failure.
2. **`determinism.rs`** generates every example twice and asserts the
   two runs are byte-identical.
3. **`conformance.rs`, `openapi.rs`, `golden.rs`, `tests/ui/`** — the
   behavioral and negative corpora.
4. **`scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh`** — 50 program×target combinations
   that *run* the generated code.

The relationship between this arc and layer 2 deserves stating plainly,
because it is unusual and it is a trap. In v0.32, `determinism.rs` was
purely a **safety net** — a test that would catch that arc's M6
indexing work if it leaked `HashMap` iteration order into a code path.
In this arc, `determinism.rs` is simultaneously the safety net **and
the primary optimization target**. It is the single most expensive
binary in the suite and the one M3 rewrites.

That dual role is the precise mechanism by which Contract B could be
breached without anyone intending it. The fastest way to make
`determinism.rs` quick is to make its second generation cheap; the
second generation existing at all is the entire point of the test.
Every review of M3's diff asks one question first — *does this still
generate twice, from scratch, and compare?* — before it asks anything
about speed.

Layer 4 is the one that costs minutes and is most tempting to skip on
a milestone that "obviously cannot change output." It is not skippable
here either, and the reason is specific to this arc: **a concurrency
bug does not fail the way a logic bug fails.** A torn jar read (the
race M2 fixes) or a scratch-directory collision would produce a
*corrupt or truncated generated file*, intermittently, on some runs
and not others. That is exactly the failure a single snapshot diff on a
lucky run does not catch and an executing corpus does.

## Pillar 3 — The whole suite runs at every milestone boundary, not only at the arc's close

Unchanged from v0.32 Pillar 3, and for the same reason: it is the only
way Contract C is enforceable, since a trade discovered at close-out
cannot be attributed to the milestone that made it.

The sequence runs **twice per milestone** — once before the milestone's
first commit, confirming a clean starting point, and once after its
work lands.

```sh
# Correctness first. A fast milestone that changes output is a failure,
# and there is no point measuring a tree that is already wrong.
cargo fmt --all && cargo clippy --workspace --all-targets -- -D warnings
cargo test --workspace                               # 520 tests, 0 failures
git status --porcelain -- tests/tests/snapshots/     # must be empty
find . -name '*.snap.new' -not -path './target/*'    # must be empty
scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh                             # 50 combos, all PASS

# Then the measurement.
cargo build --release -p ciac
cargo run --release -p ciac-integration-tests --bin ciac-bench -- \
    --compare=docs/perf/baseline.json --with-slow-tests
cargo test -p ciac-integration-tests --test perf_budget
```

**This arc inverts v0.32's cost calculus for the `--with-slow-tests`
arm.** That document deliberately confined `--with-verify`,
`--with-slow-tests` and `--with-sim` to M8, on the grounds that they
were M8's own subject and cost roughly thirteen minutes per boundary to
measure things no other milestone touched. Here, `--with-slow-tests`
**is** the arc's headline metric and runs at every boundary;
`--with-verify` and `--with-sim` are the ones that would be overhead,
and they run at M1, M7 and M9 only. `--with-callgrind` and
`--with-scaling` are not run per-boundary at all: no Rust code path
changes in this arc, so their instruction counts and growth ratios are
expected to be flat, and confirming that twice is sufficient (M1 and
M7). If either moves, something unintended happened — see Pillar 6.

## Pillar 4 — Two baselines, so that no delta is ever ambiguous

Inherited from v0.32 Pillar 4, which stated the reasoning completely: a
single rolling baseline answers "what did this milestone do" and loses
"what has the arc done"; a single frozen baseline answers the reverse.

- **`docs/perf/baseline-v0.30.0.json`** — frozen at M1, never
  rewritten for the arc's duration, committed with a message stating
  that immutability explicitly so a later reader finding it unchanged
  knows it was intent rather than neglect. Every cumulative delta and
  M9's closing table are measured against it.
- **`docs/perf/baseline.json`** — the existing rolling file,
  regenerated after each **kept** milestone and never after a reverted
  one, so the next milestone's `--compare` shows its own incremental
  delta rather than the arc's accumulated one.

Every `--update-baseline` carries its justification in the commit
message, per the standing discipline in `docs/perf/README.md`.

**One methodological requirement specific to this arc, and it is not
optional: every timing in it is a 3-rep median taken in isolation.**
Wall-clock measurements of a parallel workload on a 4-vCPU machine are
far noisier than the instruction counts and DHAT byte counts that
carried most of v0.32's thresholds — callgrind is deterministic and
carries no run-to-run noise at all, which is why that arc could lean on
single readings for several milestones. This arc cannot. v0.32's own M5
recorded a `determinism` reading of +8.56% that turned out to be a
measurement artifact — a single-shot reading taken immediately after
two heavy phases in the same process, which three isolated re-runs
contradicted outright. That trap is directly in this arc's path,
because *every* number here is a wall clock on the exact binaries that
misread. Nothing else may be running; the machine gets a moment to
settle between reps; the median is reported, and the spread is reported
alongside it whenever it exceeds 5%.

## Pillar 5 — Rollback is graduated, and pre-registered

Inherited from v0.32 Pillar 5, including its argument for the
interesting rule — that a change landing 3% against a 10% threshold has
not failed in any obvious way, but has permanently added a code path,
an invariant, and a paragraph someone will read in two years, to buy a
number smaller than the noise on some of the metrics involved.

| Condition | Action |
|---|---|
| Any golden snapshot moves | **Revert.** Contract A breach. |
| `determinism.rs` fails | **Revert.** Contract A breach. |
| Assertion/invocation count drops | **Revert.** Contract B breach. |
| Any test becomes intermittent across the ≥5 repeat runs | **Revert.** Contract D breach — an intermittent test is worse than a slow one. |
| `scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh` fails | **Revert.** Contract A, behavioral layer. |
| `perf_budget.rs` fails | **Revert.** The one currently-blocking gate. |
| `perf_baseline.rs` reports >1% instruction growth | **Revert**, *and investigate* — see Pillar 6, this should be structurally impossible. |
| Any unrelated metric regresses beyond its noise band | **Revert**, unless the trade is stated and accepted in the milestone report. |
| Gain < ½ the pre-registered threshold | **Revert.** The complexity is not paid for. |
| Gain ≥ ½ but < the threshold | **Keep**, and record the shortfall explicitly — **and measure the proposed root cause before recording it** (Pillar 1). |
| Gain ≥ threshold | **Keep.** |

**On ratio-shaped thresholds specifically** (M3–M5's `≥N×` speedups):
"gain" means the *speedup achieved over baseline*, not the raw ratio
number, since a ratio threshold's zero point is 1.0× (no change), not
0. For a `≥T×` threshold the half-threshold point is `(T + 1) / 2` —
for M3's `≥1.8×`, half is **1.4×**; for M4's `≥1.6×`, half is **1.3×**;
for M5's `≥1.5×`, half is **1.25×**. (An earlier draft of M3 stated its
own half-point as "0.9×," which is incoherent for a speedup ratio —
below 1.0× is a slowdown, not half a gain — and is corrected here
rather than left to propagate into M4/M5's identically-shaped
thresholds.)

The mechanics: each milestone is one commit where possible (M2 and M6
are two — implementation, then the test or measurement proving it). A
rollback is `git revert` of that milestone's commits, pushed, with the
outcome recorded in this document's running log rather than silently
dropped. A reverted milestone leaves behind a measured negative result,
which is a genuine contribution to the next arc's inventory.

The row that has already been exercised once, in the arc this document
corrects, is the "≥ ½ but < threshold" row — and it is exercised
*wrongly* when the recorded explanation is a guess. That is now
covered by its own clause above.

## Pillar 6 — The instruments that should not move are as informative as the ones that should

v0.32's Pillar 6 was "fix the data structure, not the symptom," which
had a clear referent there (the un-indexed `SystemGraph`) and has none
here — this arc changes no data structure. The slot is more usefully
spent on something this arc genuinely needs.

Three instruments are expected to be **completely flat** across every
milestone, and their flatness is a load-bearing check rather than a
formality:

- **`perf_baseline.rs`** (callgrind instruction counts on `ciac
  check`/`ciac build`). Valgrind does not follow the `java` child
  process, and no Rust code path in the compiler changes. A movement
  here means something unintended happened — most plausibly that M6's
  flag change accidentally altered a path taken during a Rust-side
  build, or that a milestone touched compiler code it was not supposed
  to touch.
- **`perf_scaling.rs`** (growth ratios for `sema::analyze` and
  friends). Same reasoning; no IR or sema code is in scope.
- **`ciac-bench`'s per-phase generation timings** for python, rust and
  typescript. These backends never invoke the JVM at all, so AppCDS
  cannot touch them and thread-scoping a *test harness* cannot either.

**`perf_budget.rs` is the one gate expected to move, and it moves in
the safe direction.** It compares java's `generate()` cost against the
median backend's. AppCDS lowers java's *absolute* cost, which lowers
the ratio, which *increases* headroom against the gate's multiplier.
v0.32's M9 left roughly 2× headroom and flagged that a future arc
cutting the *cheap* backends' cost would need to revisit the
multiplier; this arc cuts the *expensive* one instead, pushing the
ratio the other way. That is the benign direction, but "expected to be
benign" is not "confirmed benign," and M7 records the actual number
either way. Tightening the multiplier to reclaim the new headroom is
explicitly **not** in scope — that is a ratchet decision for whoever
owns the next arc, taken with a soak behind it, not a side effect of a
test-suite optimization.

## Pillar 7 — The checkpoint has real teeth, including the authority to stop

M7 is a hard serialization point: no work under M8 or M9 lands before
M7's own commit does.

Its pre-registered outcomes:

- **(a) Proceed.** Cumulative gain clears the arc's −30% headline and
  no contract is breached. M8 and M9 run as written.
- **(b) Proceed narrowed.** One milestone underperformed and is
  reverted; the rest ship. M8's documentation work adjusts to describe
  what actually landed.
- **(c) Stop and ship what exists.** If M3 and M4 alone clear the
  headline — plausible, since they address the two largest binaries —
  M5's `insta` restructuring and/or M6's archive may not be worth
  their complexity, and the arc closes early. This is recorded as a
  legitimate success, not a fallback: `30UpdatePlan.md` M5 stopped an
  arc early on exactly these grounds and was right to, and
  `32UpdatePlan.md` Pillar 7 put it well — "a checkpoint that cannot
  stop the work is a status meeting."
- **(d) Stop and revert.** Concurrency proves flaky in a way the ≥5
  repeat runs surface but cannot be pinned down. The measured result
  and the flakiness evidence go in the running log, and the arc closes
  having established that the approach does not survive contact with
  this suite. This outcome is unlikely and is written down anyway,
  because the outcome nobody wrote down is the one that gets argued
  about at the time.

## Pillar 8 — Concurrency is pinned by a test before it is introduced

New in this arc, and the reason Contract D exists.

Every previous arc's characteristic risk was a *deterministic* wrong
answer: a stale index, a mis-threaded hash, a template resolved by a
different loader. Those fail the same way every time, which means the
existing suite either catches them immediately or never — and "catches
them immediately" is what actually happened, repeatedly.

This arc's characteristic risk is different in kind. A torn jar read, a
scratch-directory collision, an `insta` assertion racing another
thread's pending-snapshot state — each fails **on some runs and not
others**, at a rate that depends on machine load, core count, and
scheduling luck. A green `cargo test --workspace` is close to no
evidence at all against this class of bug. Worse, the natural
development loop actively hides it: the failure rate on a quiet
4-vCPU machine running one binary at a time is far lower than on a
loaded CI runner, so the bug ships and then appears in someone else's
pipeline as an unreproducible flake.

Three consequences, all binding:

1. **M2 lands first, and its ordering is not negotiable.** The atomic
   jar materialization and the N-thread identity test exist *before*
   M3 spawns the first worker thread. Fixing a known race after
   introducing the concurrency that exercises it is how a flake gets
   discovered by someone who does not know this document exists.
2. **The exit checklist requires ≥5 consecutive clean runs** of each
   affected binary, not one. This is the cheapest available
   approximation of a soak, and it is the checklist item most likely
   to be skipped under time pressure, which is why it is stated as an
   absolute rather than left to judgment.
3. **Uncertain parallelization defaults to serial.** Inherited from
   v0.32's open question 4, whose rule was exactly right and
   generalizes: "a wrong parallelization here produces a flaky
   validation failure, which is worse than a slow one." M5 applies
   this directly — `insta` assertions stay on the main thread rather
   than being made thread-safe, giving up some of the available win to
   keep the semantics unarguable.

## Determinism and supply chain

**No new dependencies.** Not one crate is added to `Cargo.toml`; no new
entry appears in `Cargo.lock` except the version bump's own
regeneration at M9. `deny.toml` is untouched and
`cargo-audit`/`cargo-deny` gain no new surface.

The milestone that would obviously want a dependency is M3, where
`rayon` is the reach any reviewer will think of first. It is not taken,
for three reasons stated in descending order of force. First,
precedent: `crates/ciac/src/commands.rs:3626` already records the
decision in this workspace, in a comment, next to the code that
implements the alternative. Second, sufficiency: the workload is 29
independent items chunked across ≤4 workers with no work-stealing
requirement and no nested parallelism, which `std::thread::scope`
covers completely. Third, proportion: adding a thread-pool dependency
to a deliberately small tree in order to speed up a *test harness* is
the weakest possible justification for a supply-chain expansion.

**Compiler determinism is not at risk in this arc, and it is worth
being precise about why**, since "we added threads" and "output is
deterministic" sound like they should be in tension. They are not,
because the threads are added *outside* the compiler. Each worker
thread runs an independent, self-contained `generate()` on its own
example and produces an owned `GeneratedProject`; no two workers share
mutable state, and no worker's output depends on any other's. The only
ordering that could become nondeterministic is the order in which
*results* are collected, and that matters in exactly one place —
`golden.rs`'s snapshot assertions — which is precisely why M5 sorts
results back into today's iteration order before asserting rather than
asserting as they arrive.

AppCDS deserves the same explicit treatment. A class-data-sharing
archive changes *how the JVM loads the classes it was going to load
anyway*; it does not change which classes exist, what they do, or what
google-java-format emits. This was verified directly rather than
argued: the same deliberately mis-formatted input, formatted with and
without the archive, produced byte-identical output. M6 repeats that
verification as a mandatory exit criterion.

2. **M2 — Make the jar race impossible, and pin the concurrency
   contract with a test, before any concurrency exists.** Two changes,
   both correctness, neither claiming a speedup. First, replace
   `vendored_jar_path()`'s check-then-write
   (`crates/ciac-backend-java/src/lib.rs:992`) with a write to a
   unique temporary path followed by `std::fs::rename` onto the final
   path — atomic within a filesystem, so concurrent callers observe
   either the complete old file or the complete new one and never a
   torn prefix. Keep the existing length check as the cheap fast path
   so the common case still costs one `stat`. Second, add a test that
   spawns N threads (N = `available_parallelism()`, minimum 4), has
   each call `JavaBackend::generate()` on the same IR, and asserts all
   N results are byte-identical to each other and to a serial
   reference — with no panic, no formatter error, and no torn jar.
   That test is worth having independently of this arc: it pins as an
   executable contract what `format_batch.rs:69-72`'s doc comment
   currently only asserts in prose. Record in the commit message that
   `format_batch`'s `scratch_dir()` was audited and needs no change,
   and why, so the next reader does not re-audit it. **No threshold —
   correctness prerequisite.** Wall time must not regress measurably;
   the rename path adds one syscall on the cold path only. **Exit:**
   new concurrency test green and green on ≥5 consecutive runs; full
   suite green; Contract A spot-check clean.

3. **M3 — Re-cut `determinism.rs` on the axis where 95% of its time
   is.** This is the arc's headline milestone and the direct retry of
   v0.32 item 7. `generation_is_byte_deterministic_for` currently
   loops all 29 examples serially inside each per-backend test fn;
   the fix is to parallelize *that loop*. Restructure the helper to
   accept a **backend factory** (`fn() -> B where B: Backend`) rather
   than a `&dyn Backend`, so each worker thread constructs its own —
   free, since all five backends are zero-sized unit structs, and it
   is what avoids adding `Sync` to a public trait to speed up a test.
   Partition `ciac_files(&examples_dir())` into chunks across
   `available_parallelism()`, clamped to 4 (see Risks for the
   oversubscription bound), and run them under `std::thread::scope`,
   mirroring `commands.rs:3652`. **Assertions stay inside the worker
   threads** — they are pure `String` comparisons with no shared
   mutable state and no `insta` involvement, so a failure panics its
   worker and `thread::scope` propagates it on join; the existing
   messages already name example and backend, so attribution survives,
   and Risks requires verifying that deliberately rather than assuming
   it. **The double generation per example is preserved verbatim**
   (Contract B). Rewrite the module doc: it currently presents the
   per-backend split as the fix, and must now record that by-backend
   was the wrong axis, that java is 95.3% of the file's cost, and that
   the by-example split *inside* each backend test is what pays.
   **Threshold: ≥1.8× on `generation_is_byte_deterministic_java`.**
   Derivation: the measured parallel ceiling is 2.26×; 1.8× is ~80% of
   it, leaving margin for scheduling overhead, chunk imbalance across
   29 items on 4 workers, and the serial front-end work each example
   still does. Under 1.4× (half the threshold's gain over 1.0×, per
   Pillar 5's ratio-threshold note) → revert per Pillar 5. **Exit:** the
   per-milestone checklist, plus the java test's 3-rep median with
   spread, plus ≥5 consecutive clean runs of the binary.

4. **M4 — The same cut, applied to `openapi.rs` and
   `conformance.rs`.** `openapi.rs` is structurally identical to
   `determinism.rs` — already split per backend, assertions pure
   (`serde_json` parsing, `$ref` resolution, `BTreeSet` membership),
   no shared state — and takes the same factory-plus-chunked-scope
   treatment with no new design questions. `conformance.rs` differs
   in shape: its three test fns (`c3`, `c4a`, `c4b`) each loop the
   examples calling `supported_projects(&ir)`, which generates **all
   five backends** for that example. Parallelize the **outer example
   loop** and leave `supported_projects` alone, so each worker owns
   one example's five projects end to end and no two workers touch the
   same IR. Note that libtest is already running the three test fns
   concurrently, so this nests — which is why the worker clamp from M3
   applies here too and why Risks bounds the total. **Threshold:
   ≥1.6× each.** Derivation: lower than M3's because both files do
   materially more non-java work per example than `determinism` does
   (openapi parses and walks every spec; conformance generates four
   cheap backends alongside java), so the serial fraction each retains
   is larger and Amdahl caps them lower — the same arithmetic that
   capped item 7, applied honestly in advance this time. **Exit:**
   checklist, both medians, ≥5 clean runs each.

5. **M5 — `golden.rs`, where the obvious pattern is unsafe and a
   different one is required.** `insta` is the complication.
   `assert_snapshot!` carries thread-local pending-snapshot state and
   writes `.snap.new` files on mismatch, and
   `example_generated_project_snapshots` (`tests/tests/golden.rs:28-48`)
   asserts *inside* its loop, so the first mismatch aborts the
   remainder — behavior a naive parallelization would make
   nondeterministic, turning a reproducible "example 7 diverged" into
   "some example diverged, differently each run." The shape that
   preserves semantics exactly: **phase one, parallel** — across
   chunked examples × backends, run `check_support` + `generate()` +
   `project_dump()` and collect `(snapshot_name, dump)` pairs, which
   is where all the java cost lives; **phase two, serial, on the main
   thread** — sort the collected pairs into today's deterministic
   order (example, then backend) and run every `insta::assert_snapshot!`
   exactly as it runs now. Failure ordering is therefore identical to
   today's, not merely equivalent. Leave `example_ir_snapshots` and
   `example_graph_dot_snapshots` untouched: they never invoke a
   backend, cost almost nothing, and changing them buys nothing while
   adding review surface. **Threshold: ≥1.5×.** Derivation: lower
   again, because the serial assert phase and the two untouched
   non-codegen test fns both remain in the critical path by design —
   this milestone deliberately trades achievable speedup for
   unarguable snapshot semantics, per Pillar 8's third consequence.
   **Exit:** checklist, median, ≥5 clean runs, and explicit
   confirmation that a forced mismatch still reports the same example
   as it would today.

6. **M6 — Shrink the fixed cost itself: an AppCDS archive for the
   formatter JVM.** This revives v0.32 item 11, which that arc cut at
   M7 rather than attempting. Add `cds_archive_path()` beside
   `vendored_jar_path()`, following that function's established shape:
   a stable path in `std::env::temp_dir()`, reused across calls and
   across processes. **Key the filename on JDK version plus jar
   length**, so that a JDK upgrade or a formatter-version bump mints a
   fresh archive instead of silently falling back forever — a stale
   archive is rejected by the JVM and costs the fallback path on every
   subsequent run, which is a slow, silent regression rather than a
   visible failure. **Generate lazily when absent**, via one
   `-XX:ArchiveClassesAtExit` run over a throwaway temp `.java` file,
   using the **exact same flag set** the real invocation uses — the
   `--add-exports`/`--add-opens` block at
   `crates/ciac-backend-java/src/lib.rs:1052-1058` plus
   `-XX:TieredStopAtLevel=1` — since a flag mismatch causes the
   archive to be rejected at use time. **Write it atomically** (temp
   plus rename) and guard generation with a process-wide
   `OnceLock`/`Mutex`: this is load-bearing given M3–M5, because
   without it N worker threads each try to mint a ~12.6 MB archive
   simultaneously on first use. **Treat every failure as non-fatal** —
   if minting fails, proceed without CDS and surface nothing, which is
   safe because `-XX:SharedArchiveFile` against a missing or corrupt
   archive was verified to exit 0. Add the flag to the existing
   `cmd.arg(...)` chain and extend the comment block at `:1032-1051`
   in its own established style: state the measured numbers, state
   that this is a startup-shape flag that cannot change an emitted
   byte, and — matching that block's treatment of `-XX:+UseSerialGC` —
   state what was tried and rejected. **Two thresholds, both
   pre-registered. (i) ≥25% reduction in the formatter's fixed cost**,
   measured as the 1-file invocation from M1's control (measured 34%:
   0.638s → 0.420s; 25% leaves margin for JDK and filesystem
   variation). **(ii) ≤1.5s first-run penalty** on a cold machine with
   no archive present — the honest cost of shipping this default-on
   rather than behind an opt-in env var, and the number that decides
   whether that decision survives. Exceeding (ii) converts the feature
   to opt-in within the same milestone and is recorded as a deviation,
   not a design choice. **Extra exit criterion, non-negotiable:
   byte-identity of formatter output re-verified explicitly** — format
   the same deliberately mis-formatted input with and without the
   archive and confirm `cmp` reports identical — because this is the
   only milestone in the arc that changes how the formatter itself
   runs, and Contract A's other layers would catch a systematic
   change but are weaker against a subtle one.

### Per-milestone exit checklists

Every milestone that touches code, without exception, before its work
is considered shipped:

- `cargo fmt --all && cargo clippy --workspace --all-targets -- -D warnings`
- `cargo test --workspace` green — **the full workspace**, not only the
  binary the milestone touched.
- **Contract A check.** `git status --porcelain -- tests/tests/snapshots/`
  empty; zero `*.snap.new` outside `target/`; `scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh`
  reporting all 50 combinations PASS. If any is false, the milestone
  stops and the cause is investigated as a defect before anything else
  proceeds — a moved snapshot is never accepted, only explained.
- **Contract B check.** The diff is reviewed specifically for assertion
  count, input count, and invocation count. For M3 the question is
  asked explicitly and answered in the milestone report: *does this
  still generate every example twice, from scratch, and compare?*
- **Contract C check.** `ciac-bench --compare` reviewed in full, not
  only the milestone's own metric.
- **Contract D check.** **≥5 consecutive clean runs** of every binary
  the milestone made concurrent. One green run is not evidence.
- **Threshold check** against this document's pre-registered number,
  as a 3-rep median taken in isolation, with the spread reported when
  it exceeds 5%.
- **Pillar 5 decision recorded explicitly**, including when the
  outcome is the boring one. A milestone report with no stated
  keep/revert verdict is the exact failure this discipline exists to
  prevent — and if the verdict is "kept, shortfall disclosed," the
  root cause offered for that shortfall carries a measurement or is
  labelled a hypothesis (Pillar 1).
- Numbers land in `docs/perf/baseline.json` (kept milestones) or the
  revert commit's message (reverted ones) and in this document's
  running log — never only in a transcript nobody will re-read.

## Open questions resolved at implementation (pre-registered)

Recorded now, with their decision rules, so the answers cannot be
reverse-engineered from whatever turns out to be convenient. The first
three were put to the requester during planning and went unanswered;
they are resolved here on the recommended option, with the cost of
each decision stated rather than buried.

1. **Does AppCDS ship default-on, or behind an opt-in env var?**
   Resolved: **default-on**, for both the test suite and `ciac build`.
   The archive is minted once per machine per (JDK version, jar) pair
   and reused by every subsequent process, and `-XX:SharedArchiveFile`
   is verified fail-soft in both failure modes, so the downside is
   bounded and silent. **The cost, stated plainly: the first java
   build on a cold machine pays roughly +0.6s** to mint the archive,
   and a developer who builds java exactly once on a fresh container
   pays that cost for no return. **Reversal rule:** if M6's measured
   first-run penalty exceeds 1.5s, the feature converts to an opt-in
   env var within the same milestone, and the conversion is recorded
   as a deviation rather than presented as the plan.

2. **Does this arc also fix `conformance.rs`'s redundant
   generation?** Resolved: **no** — see Explicit cuts for the full
   reasoning and the measurement that makes it tempting. The rule that
   settles it: this arc's changes are all *scheduling* changes, which
   preserve test independence by construction; memoizing across test
   fns is a *semantic* change to how independent those tests are.
   Mixing the two in one arc would make a single regression ambiguous
   between "we parallelized wrong" and "we shared state wrong," which
   is exactly the attribution problem Contract C exists to prevent.

3. **Is `plans/32UpdatePlan.md`'s M8 note edited or annotated?**
   Resolved: **annotated**, with a dated correction appended beneath
   the original and the original left untouched. Rewriting it would
   destroy the record of what was believed at the time and why, which
   is the more useful half of the artifact — a plan series whose
   errors are silently corrected teaches nothing about how they
   happened.

4. **What is the right worker count, and does it need tuning per
   binary?** Resolved in advance: **`available_parallelism()` clamped
   to 4**, uniformly, no per-binary tuning. The measurement supporting
   the clamp is that 8 concurrent formatter invocations on 4 vCPUs
   held the same ~2.3× as 4 did — additional concurrency bought
   nothing, so a low cap costs nothing and bounds the blast radius
   under libtest's own nesting. **Rule:** if a milestone misses its
   threshold and raising the clamp is proposed as the fix, the clamp
   may be raised **only** with a measurement showing the higher value
   helps *and* a re-run of Contract D's ≥5 clean runs at the new
   value. Tuning concurrency upward to rescue a threshold is precisely
   how an intermittent failure gets shipped.

5. **Do worker-thread panics still attribute failures correctly?**
   Must be answered by demonstration at M3, not by reading the code.
   `thread::scope` propagates a worker panic on join, and the existing
   assertion messages already carry example and backend — but "should
   propagate legibly" and "does propagate legibly" are different
   claims, and this arc has one running example of the difference.
   **Rule:** M3 temporarily corrupts one expectation, confirms the
   failure output still names the responsible example and backend,
   and reverts the corruption. If attribution degrades, M3 collects
   results and asserts serially — M5's shape — rather than shipping a
   suite whose failures are harder to read than the ones it replaced.

6. **Does the AppCDS archive need to be keyed on anything beyond JDK
   version and jar length?** Unknown, and deliberately not guessed.
   The two known invalidators are covered. **Rule:** if M6 observes
   the JVM rejecting a freshly minted archive for any reason other
   than a flag mismatch, the key gains whatever field explains the
   rejection before the milestone closes — a silently rejected
   archive costs the fallback path forever and reports nothing, which
   makes it the worst available failure shape for this feature.

## Verification strategy

- **Pillar 3's suite at every milestone boundary**, before and after,
  recorded per the exit checklist. This is the arc's primary
  verification mechanism.
- **`scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh` at every milestone**, with no "this one
  is obviously safe" carve-out. It is the only layer that executes
  generated code, and the concurrency failures this arc risks —
  truncated or interleaved file content — are exactly the shape that
  produces code which still parses and still diffs clean against a
  snapshot on a lucky run.
- **`determinism.rs` treated as its own gate at M3**, not merely part
  of the suite, on the same reasoning v0.32 applied to it at M6: it is
  the test most likely to catch this arc's characteristic failure, and
  it is also the file M3 rewrites, which is the tension Pillar 2
  describes.
- **≥5 consecutive clean runs of every concurrent binary**, per
  Contract D. This is the single most important verification step in
  the arc and the one with the weakest natural incentive to perform.
- **Cold-cache and corrupt-archive paths exercised explicitly at M6.**
  Delete the archive and confirm the first run mints it, succeeds, and
  produces identical bytes; truncate the archive and confirm the run
  still exits 0 with correct formatting.
- **CI polled to a terminal state after every push.** Known-failing
  jobs inherited from before this arc are disclosed and excluded; any
  *new* failure is a Pillar 5 rollback trigger, not a flake to re-run.
  A newly *intermittent* CI job is treated as a Contract D failure
  even if a re-run goes green — that is what the failure looks like.
- **M9's cumulative accounting** against `baseline-v0.30.0.json`:
  every metric, with the arc's total delta stated per metric rather
  than as a single headline.

## Milestone dependencies and parallelism

```
M1 ──▶ M2 ──┬──▶ M3 ──┐
            ├──▶ M4 ──┤
            ├──▶ M5 ──┼──▶ M7 (CHECKPOINT) ──▶ M8 ──▶ M9
            └──▶ M6 ──┘
```

**M2 must precede M3, M4 and M5, and the ordering is not negotiable.**
It is the only hard prerequisite in the arc, and Pillar 8 states the
reasoning: M2 fixes a race that today is merely latent and that M3–M5
would make hot. Fixing it afterwards means the window between M3 and
M2 is a window in which an intermittent, hard-to-attribute failure can
be introduced and then blamed on the parallelization rather than on
the jar write.

**M2 must also precede M6**, for a narrower reason: M6 introduces a
second lazily-materialized artifact in `temp_dir()` with exactly the
same check-then-write shape as the jar. It is written to use the
atomic pattern M2 establishes rather than inventing a second one, and
having the pattern in the tree first is what makes that automatic
rather than a review comment.

**M3, M4, M5 and M6 are mutually independent** — different files,
different mechanisms, no shared state. M6 in particular is independent
of the parallelism work entirely: it would pay off even if M3–M5 were
all reverted, since it shrinks a fixed cost that 203 `generate()` calls
each pay regardless of how they are scheduled. They could proceed in
any order or concurrently, but each still takes its own full suite run
at its own boundary, because Contract C is only enforceable if a
regression can be attributed to one milestone.

**M7 is a hard serialization point** with the authority to stop the
arc (Pillar 7). M8 and M9 are documentation and close-out and follow
strictly.

## Explicit cuts

- **No `conformance.rs` memoization — the highest-value follow-up, and
  it is being left on the table deliberately.** Its three test fns
  (`c3`, `c4a`, `c4b`) each independently call `supported_projects(&ir)`
  for every example, generating all five backends every time: 87 java
  `generate()` calls where 29 would do. Since `generate()` is provably
  deterministic — that is precisely what `determinism.rs` asserts — a
  shared cache would be sound, and the available win is a further
  ~2–3× on this binary's 52.53s. It is cut here because it is a
  *semantic* change (test independence) rather than a *scheduling*
  change, and mixing the two makes any regression ambiguous between
  them. **Recorded as the single highest-value item for the next
  arc's inventory**, with its measurement attached so nobody has to
  rediscover it.
- **No persistent or pooled formatter JVM.** This was v0.32 item 11's
  most ambitious form, and M6 takes its narrow form instead. A
  long-lived formatter process needs a wire protocol, a lifecycle, a
  crash-recovery story and a shutdown path, shipped inside a compiler
  that currently invokes a stock jar and exits. After M3–M6 the
  remaining headroom does not justify that surface, and a bespoke Java
  server is not a thing this workspace should own to speed up tests.
- **No formatter memoization by content hash.** It would roughly halve
  `determinism.rs` immediately and is the most tempting cut on this
  list. It is also a direct Contract B violation: the second
  generation would stop exercising the formatter, which is the thing
  the second generation exists to exercise. Named here explicitly so
  that "we could just cache it" is a settled question rather than a
  recurring suggestion.
- **No new gates.** See "What this arc does not do."
- **No `rayon`.** See "Determinism and supply chain."
- **No `perf_budget.rs` ratchet tightening**, despite M6 creating
  headroom that would invite it. See Pillar 6.
- **No compiler changes.** No template, context struct, or codegen
  decision is touched, which is what makes Contract A cheap to hold
  and any snapshot movement unambiguously a bug.

## Risks

- **Oversubscription, and it nests two levels deep.** libtest already
  runs the per-backend test fns concurrently (5 for `determinism` and
  `openapi`, 3 for `conformance`), and cargo runs test *binaries*
  serially. Adding per-example workers inside that means up to `5 × N`
  runnable JVMs on 4 vCPUs. Mitigation: clamp workers to
  `available_parallelism()` capped at 4 rather than spawning
  per-example, on the measurement that 8-way concurrency gained
  nothing over 4-way — so the cap costs nothing and bounds the blast
  radius. Open question 4 pre-registers the rule for raising it.
- **An intermittent test is worse than a slow one, and this arc's
  failures are intermittent by nature.** This is Pillar 8's whole
  subject. Mitigation is ordered: M2's fix and test land first; ≥5
  consecutive clean runs per affected binary; uncertain
  parallelization defaults to serial; and a newly flaky CI job counts
  as a failure even when a re-run is green.
- **Failure attribution could degrade.** A worker panic surfaces
  through `thread::scope`'s join rather than directly, and a test
  suite whose failures are harder to read than before has made the
  project worse in a way no benchmark reports. Mitigated by open
  question 5's mandatory demonstration at M3, with M5's
  collect-then-assert shape as the pre-registered fallback.
- **`insta` and threads.** Mitigated structurally in M5 by never
  asserting off the main thread, at a deliberate cost in achievable
  speedup.
- **A silently rejected AppCDS archive.** The failure mode with the
  worst shape in the arc: it reports nothing, passes every test, and
  simply stops delivering the speedup — potentially long after the
  milestone that measured it. Mitigated by keying on JDK version and
  jar length, by using the identical flag set at mint and use time,
  and by open question 6's rule. Bounded by the verified fail-soft
  behavior, which guarantees the cost is a lost optimization rather
  than a broken build.
- **Disk.** The archive is ~12.6 MB in `temp_dir()`, alongside the
  existing 3.6 MB jar. v0.32's M8 already recorded a disk-exhaustion
  incident in this environment (a 15 GB `target/debug` after repeated
  before/after rebuild cycles), and this arc's methodology — 3 reps
  plus ≥5 repeat runs per milestone — is rebuild-heavy in the same
  way. Not a blocker; worth a sentence in the docs and worth watching
  during M7's full sweep.
- **Every ratio here was measured on this machine.** 2.26× is what 4
  vCPUs give. On a 16-core runner the parallel win would be larger and
  AppCDS's *share* of the remaining time correspondingly smaller. All
  thresholds are stated as ratios for this reason — and this arc has
  more standing than most to insist on the distinction, since it
  exists to correct a conclusion that misapplied exactly this caveat.

## Confidence and handoff

This document is written whole, before M1's own commit, per the
standing precedent that a plan is authored and then executed milestone
by milestone rather than discovered as it goes. Every threshold above
was chosen before the code that must clear it exists.

The arc is deliberately smaller than its predecessor: six working
milestones against nine, two inventory rows against twelve, and a
footprint that touches the compiler in one file. That is not modesty
about the payoff — the headline is a ~60% cut to the slowest part of
the developer loop, larger in proportion than anything v0.32 achieved
on a single metric. It is a judgment that this particular win is
available *cleanly*, and that the way to lose it is to entangle it
with a codegen change that makes a moved snapshot ambiguous.

The handoff to the next arc is three things. First, a corrected
inventory: item 7 has a real result rather than a disclosed shortfall
attached to a wrong explanation, and item 11 has a shipped narrow form
rather than a cut. Second, a measured, un-taken opportunity —
`conformance.rs`'s 3× redundant generation, with the reasoning for
deferring it and the estimate attached, which is worth strictly more
than an absent row. Third, and least tangible but most useful: a
worked example of a plan series catching its own error. v0.32 recorded
its shortfall openly and reasoned about it in good faith and still got
it wrong, because the reasoning ran ahead of the measurement by about
ninety seconds of work. The correction was only possible *because* the
number, the threshold, and the explanation were all written down in
the same place. That is the argument for this entire practice, and it
is now evidenced rather than asserted.
