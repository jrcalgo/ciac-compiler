# CIaC v0.34-file — The Harness That Must Still Be Able to Fail: Thirteen Minutes Down to Three, Without Ever Reporting a Green It Did Not Earn (implementation plan)

> Implementation plan. Document number ≠ release number (standing
> precedent; expected to ship as **0.32.0**). Assumes
> `26UpdatePlan.md`–`33UpdatePlan.md` shipped, the last of them as
> 0.31.0.
>
> This arc has exactly one subject: `scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh`, the 50
> program×target harness that is simultaneously **the strongest
> correctness instrument this repository owns** and **the slowest thing
> a developer is ever asked to run on demand**. It is the only layer
> that *executes* generated code rather than diffing it, which is why
> `33UpdatePlan.md` required it at every milestone boundary and ran it
> five separate times over that arc's execution. It is also explicitly
> not wired into CI — `.github/workflows/ci.yml:514` and `:551` both
> annotate it "(not CI-invoked, run on demand)" — so every second it
> costs is a human being waiting at a terminal, and a 13-minute
> correctness gate is a correctness gate developers learn to skip.
>
> Two levers are implemented here, both measured before this document
> was written. The first is nearly free and reuses a helper that
> already exists. The second is worth more and carries a failure mode
> that would **silently destroy the script's entire purpose** — a
> careless implementation reports "all combinations green" while
> combinations are failing, because the failure counters live in shell
> variables that backgrounded subshells copy rather than share. That
> asymmetry — a large win guarded by one catastrophic, *silent* failure
> shape — is the whole reason this arc gets a plan document instead of
> a patch, and it is why the accounting rewrite lands and is proven
> correct at M3 **before** any concurrency exists at M4.
>
> **Contract A — the script's verdict is preserved exactly.** The same
> 50 program×target combinations, driving the same scenarios, producing
> the same `[PASS]`/`[FAIL]` per scenario, with the same exit-code
> semantics. A run that would fail today must fail after this arc, for
> the same reason, naming the same combination. This is the contract
> every other one exists to protect: the script's output *is* its
> product.
>
> **Contract B — no generated byte changes.** Every generated byte, on
> every target, for every example, is identical before and after. Lever
> 1 changes *where cargo caches build artifacts*; lever 2 changes *the
> order in which independent subprocesses are scheduled*. Neither
> touches a template, a context struct, or any codegen decision. The
> enforcement mechanism is unchanged and is not modified by this arc:
> the 145 golden snapshots
> (`tests/tests/snapshots/golden__gen__{python,rust,typescript,go,java}__*.snap`),
> `determinism.rs`, `conformance.rs`, `openapi.rs`, `golden.rs`, the
> `tests/ui/` negative corpus, and `sim-corpus-x5.sh` itself. Any
> snapshot movement at all is a bug, with no disclosed exception
> anywhere in this arc.
>
> **Contract C — no metric may regress to buy another.** M2 touches
> compiler code (`crates/ciac/src/commands.rs`), so unlike a
> script-only change it owes the full `ciac-bench --compare` sweep at
> its boundary, reviewed in its entirety rather than only on the
> targeted row. A milestone that improves its own metric while pushing
> another outside its noise band is a trade, and the trade must be
> stated and accepted at that milestone rather than discovered at
> close-out.
>
> **Contract D — the script must still be able to fail, and this is
> proven, not assumed.** New to this arc and central to it. The
> characteristic failure here is not a wrong answer; it is a harness
> that reports success while broken, which is undetectable by every
> instrument the repository has *except* a deliberate attempt to make
> it fail. Therefore: **every milestone boundary runs a forced-failure
> test** that injects a failing combination and confirms the script
> exits non-zero with correct, correctly-united counts. A milestone
> that cannot demonstrate this does not pass, regardless of what its
> timing result says. There is no version of "the numbers look good"
> that substitutes for this check.
>
> **Confidence:** high on M2 — the mechanism is measured (3.2×), the
> change reuses an existing helper almost verbatim, the blast radius is
> one function pair, and byte-identity of results was verified directly
> rather than argued. High on M3's *correctness* and moderate on its
> *tedium* — rewriting a tally onto status files is not difficult, but
> it is the milestone where a subtle mistake is least visible, which is
> exactly why it ships alone, before concurrency, with a forced-failure
> proof. Moderate on M4: the mechanism is simple and the axis was
> chosen specifically to avoid toolchain contention, but this arc
> introduces **the repository's first bash-parallelism pattern**
> (verified: no `&`, `wait`, or `xargs -P` anywhere under `scripts/`),
> so there is no in-repo precedent to copy and the review bar is
> correspondingly higher. Moderate-to-low on M5's disk work
> specifically — disk is a shared finite resource and FM5 is honestly
> labelled *mitigated*, not eliminated. Every number below was measured
> on this machine (4 vCPU, 16 GB) — **ratios are expected to travel to
> other hardware; absolute timings are not**, and no threshold in this
> document is stated as an absolute for that reason.

## The gap this version closes

`sim-corpus-x5.sh` runs 10 example programs against 5 targets — 50
combinations, each one a full `ciac sim` invocation that generates a
project, builds it with the target's real toolchain, and runs its
simulator against one or more scenario files. The loop
(`scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh:64-100`) is entirely serial.

Timed this session, one program (`quickstart.ciac`) across all five
targets:

| Target | Time | Share |
|---|---|---|
| **rust** | **48.3s** | **63%** |
| typescript | 9.8s | 13% |
| python | 7.9s | 10% |
| java | 7.5s | 10% |
| go | 3.2s | 4% |
| **total** | **76.7s** | |

At 76.4s per program × 10 programs, that is **~764s ≈ 12.7 minutes**,
consistent with the 13–18 minutes observed across this session's five
separate runs of the script.

**Rust is 63% of it, and the cause is structural rather than
algorithmic.** `sim_drive_rust_single`
(`crates/ciac/src/commands.rs:1775`) runs `cargo build -q --bin
sim_runner` inside each generated project directory. It sets no
`CARGO_TARGET_DIR`. The script then deletes that directory
(`:98`) as soon as the combination's result is captured — deliberately,
with a comment (`:92-97`) recording that "an early run of this script
hit exactly that [disk exhaustion] on its last combination."

The consequence: **the entire dependency tree — axum, tokio, serde and
the rest — is compiled from scratch ten times and discarded ten times
within a single run.** Nothing is shared between combinations that
differ only in which example program they generate from, even though
every generated `Cargo.toml` pins the same dependency versions from the
same fixed template.

### The fix already exists, one command over

`ciac verify` does not have this problem. `shared_cargo_target_dir()`
(`commands.rs:3743`) returns
`std::env::temp_dir().join("ciac-verify-cargo-target")`, and
`apply_validate_env` (`:3750-3757`) applies it to `cargo` steps,
guarded by `std::env::var_os("CARGO_TARGET_DIR").is_none()` so a
caller-set value is never overridden. That helper's own doc comment
(`:3733-3742`) already states the entire argument this arc needs:

> …every generated project's `Cargo.toml` pins the same dependency
> versions (they come from the same fixed template) — so pointing
> repeat `cargo` invocations at one shared, persistent target dir lets
> them reuse already-built dependency crates, across both repeat runs
> of the same project and different generated projects alike. Cargo's
> own target-dir locking makes concurrent use from more than one
> `cargo test` invocation at once safe, not only fast run one at a
> time.

`ciac sim` simply never received the same treatment. This is an
inconsistency between two sibling commands that both compile generated
Rust projects, not a design decision — and closing it is lever 1.

### What lever 1 is worth, measured

Five different rust programs run in sequence against one shared target
directory:

| Program | Time | Note |
|---|---|---|
| quickstart | 44.1s | cold — populates the cache |
| order-system | 34.1s | |
| query-verbs | 22.5s | |
| sim-broker-slice | 15.4s | |
| domain-orders | 15.3s | converged |

Steady state is **~15s against a 48.3s cold baseline — 3.2×**. Each
successive program benefits more as more of the shared dependency tree
lands in cache, converging once the common set is built.

### What lever 2 is worth, and why the axis matters

The loop is fully serial. Parallelizing it turns wall time from
`sum(stream)` into `max(stream)`. With lever 1 already applied:

| Stream | Total (10 programs) |
|---|---|
| **rust** | **~205s** ← critical path |
| typescript | ~98s |
| python | ~79s |
| java | ~75s |
| go | ~32s |

**The axis is the design decision, not an implementation detail.**
Parallelizing across *targets* gives each toolchain — cargo, npm, go,
maven, uv — exactly **one** concurrent user. Parallelizing across
*programs* instead would put five concurrent cargo builds on lever 1's
one shared target directory and five concurrent maven processes on one
`~/.m2`, reintroducing precisely the contention this arc is built to
avoid. The target axis is chosen for that property and this document
treats it as load-bearing (Pillar 6, FM6).

### What each target's stream actually does

The streams are not interchangeable, and knowing their shapes explains
both why the target axis is safe (FM6) and why rust alone justifies
lever 1.

| Target | Per combination, the expensive part | Cache lives where | Concurrent users under lever 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **rust** | `cargo build --bin sim_runner` — full dependency tree | per-project `target/`, **deleted after each combination** | 1 |
| typescript | `npm ci` — reinstalls `node_modules` per project | `~/.npm` (shared), `node_modules` (per project) | 1 |
| python | `uv sync` — resolves and installs into a fresh venv | uv cache in `$HOME` (shared) | 1 |
| java | `./mvnw` — compile against a warm local repo | `~/.m2` (shared) | 1 |
| go | `go build` — compile against a warm module cache | `GOCACHE`/`GOMODCACHE` (shared) | 1 |

**Rust is the only target whose cache is thrown away between
combinations.** Every other toolchain already keeps its expensive
artifacts in a shared location under `$HOME` that survives the
script's `rm -rf`. That single asymmetry is the entire explanation for
the 63%/13%/10%/10%/4% split, and it is why lever 1 is a rust-only fix
that nonetheless moves the whole arc's headline.

It also explains why the remaining four targets are left alone under
"Explicit cuts": their caches are already shared, so there is no
equivalent lever to pull. TypeScript's residual 9.8s is per-project
`node_modules` installation, which a shared cache cannot remove without
sharing `node_modules` trees between generated projects — a
substantially riskier change for a 13% share.

**And it is why the target axis keeps each cache single-writer.** The
rightmost column is the property FM6 depends on: under target-axis
parallelism every one of those shared caches sees exactly the same
access pattern it sees today under the fully serial loop. Under a
program axis, every cell in that column would read 5.

### The arithmetic this arc is answerable to

| | Wall time |
|---|---|
| Today (serial, no shared cache) | ~764s ≈ 12.7 min |
| + lever 1 (rust 480s → ~205s) | ~489s ≈ 8.2 min (**−35%**) |
| + lever 2 (wall = max, not sum) | **~205s ≈ 3.4 min (−73%)** |

## What was verified in source before this plan was written

A plan built on a profile nobody re-read is the failure mode this
series exists to prevent, and `33UpdatePlan.md` exists specifically
because a predecessor recorded a plausible-sounding root cause it had
never measured. So every headline claim below was checked against the
actual code or measured directly, and each is cited so the implementer
need not re-derive it.

**Lever 1 preserves results — verified by measurement, not by
argument.** `query-verbs` was run twice: once against a cold, private
target directory, and once against a directory already warmed by a
*different* program (`order-system`). The `[PASS]` output was
**byte-identical**. That is the assertion the script actually makes,
unchanged.

The mechanism corroborates the observation. Cargo's target directory is
a content-addressed artifact cache whose fingerprints include source
hash, feature set, profile, rustc version, and package identity
(manifest path). Two generated projects that both contain a
`sim_runner` crate resolve to distinct metadata hashes because their
manifest paths differ — which is why sharing a target dir across
unrelated projects is an explicitly supported cargo configuration
rather than a trick. A project that could not build standalone still
fails identically: an undeclared dependency is a resolution error
regardless of what happens to be cached.

**Lever 2's most obvious inherent risk does not exist.** Contention
under parallelism could in principle push individual combinations past
a wall-clock timeout, converting a speedup into flakiness. Checked
rather than assumed: the CLI path passes `wall_timeout: None`
(`commands.rs:2459`, and the `--json` variant at `:2498`). The
60-second `MCP_SIM_WALL_TIMEOUT` constant (`:2524`) is used only by the
MCP path (`:2567`). Slower individual combinations under contention
therefore cannot fail — only take longer.

**Today's output order is already nondeterministic.** `for program in
"${!PROGRAM_SCENARIOS[@]}"` (`:64`) iterates a bash associative array,
whose iteration order is hash-based rather than sorted. Verified
directly: it begins `sim-peripherals, quickstart, sim-three-service,
query-verbs, order-system`. Emitting output in a deterministic sorted
order under lever 2 is therefore a small **improvement** on the current
behavior, not merely preservation of it — and the document says so
rather than defensively claiming equivalence.

**The counters are the hazard, and they are pre-existing.** `FAILED`
and `TOTAL` (`:61-62`) are plain shell variables. `TOTAL` is
incremented once per combination (`:74`). `FAILED` is incremented both
once per errored combination (`:79`) *and* once per `[FAIL]` scenario
line (`:86`) — two different units summed into one variable, then
reported as "`$FAILED` failing combination(s) out of `$TOTAL`" (`:106`).
This is a live accounting defect independent of anything this arc does
(see FM9), and it sits in exactly the code lever 2 must rewrite.

**`scripts/` contains no bash-parallelism precedent.** Verified: no
`&`, `wait`, or `xargs -P` in any of the eight scripts
(`bench-callgrind.sh`, `bench-codegen.sh`, `bench-verify.sh`,
`check-deny-ignores.sh`, `check-guides.sh`, `sim-corpus-x5.sh`,
`sync-vendored-ciac-assets.sh`, `sync-vendored-sim.sh`). This arc
establishes the pattern, which raises the review bar and is a named
risk rather than a footnote.

**The trap handles `EXIT` only.** `trap 'rm -rf "$WORKDIR"' EXIT`
(`:29`) does not catch `INT` or `TERM`. Under today's serial design
that is a minor gap; under concurrency it becomes FM4.

**Both rust sim drivers need the lever-1 change.**
`sim_drive_rust_single` (`:1775`) builds in the project directory;
`sim_drive_rust_multi` (`:1845`) builds `system-runner/` for
multi-service programs. Three of the ten corpus programs
(`sim-three-service`, `multi-service-media`, `inventory-system`) take
the multi path, so applying the fix to only one of them would leave
30% of the rust stream uncached.

### The script today, walked through

The implementer will be rewriting roughly half of this file, so its
current anatomy is recorded here rather than left to be re-derived.
`scripts/sim-corpus-x5.sh` is 108 lines and does six things in order.

**Setup (`:19-32`).** `set -euo pipefail`, then `cd` to the repository
root relative to `BASH_SOURCE`. `TARGETS` defaults to all five and is
overridable by `SIM_CORPUS_TARGETS` or a `--targets` argument, parsed
into `TARGET_LIST` by an `IFS=','` read. `WORKDIR=$(mktemp -d)` with
`trap 'rm -rf "$WORKDIR"' EXIT`. Then the bootstrap: `cargo build -p
ciac` and `CIAC="./target/debug/ciac"`. **This pair is what FM11 would
have broken**, and the reason lever 1 lives in the CLI.

**The corpus (`:48-59`).** `declare -A PROGRAM_SCENARIOS` maps each of
ten example programs to a space-separated list of scenario files. Four
programs carry more than one scenario — `sim-peripherals` has four
(`cache-ttl`, `auth-scopes`, `http-fixtures`, `peripherals`),
`sim-vertical-slice` and `domain-orders` two each. **This is why FM9's
unit confusion produces a nonsensical message**: 50 combinations
produce substantially more than 50 scenario results, and both are
summed into one counter.

**The loop (`:64-100`).** Nested `for program` / `for target`. Per
combination it computes `out`, expands the scenario list into
`--scenario` arguments, `rm -rf "$out"`, increments `TOTAL`, then runs
the single `ciac sim` invocation (`:75`) capturing combined output into
`result`. Note the ordering: the `rm -rf` happens *before* the run
(`:73`) and again *after* (`:98`) — the second one is the disk-pressure
fix the comment at `:92-97` describes.

**Result parsing (`:81-88`).** A `while IFS= read -r line` over
`<<<"$result"` filters for lines starting `[PASS]` or `[FAIL]`, splits
each into `mark` and `name` by prefix/suffix expansion, prints a
formatted row, and conditionally increments `FAILED` via the AND-list
that is FM10.

**Error reporting (`:89-91`).** If the `ciac sim` process itself
failed, an extra row is printed with `(build/refusal)` in the scenario
column and the last line of output as the message.

**The tally (`:102-108`).** Prints a blank line, then either "all
combinations green (`$TOTAL` program×target runs)" or "`$FAILED`
failing combination(s) out of `$TOTAL`" to stderr followed by `exit 1`.

**What the rewrite must preserve exactly:** the six-column report
format including its `printf` field widths (`%-32s %-12s %-40s %s`),
the `(build/refusal)` marker for process-level failures, the header
row, the trailing blank line, the two closing messages' wording, and
`exit 1` on any failure. Contract A is checkable precisely because all
of that is mechanical.

## What this arc does not do

It does not change which combinations run. The corpus (`:48-59`), the
scenarios, and their pairings are untouched. The number 50 is the same
before and after.

It does not wire the script into CI. It stays run-on-demand; making it
a required job is a separate decision with its own cost/benefit and its
own soak, and bundling it here would conflate "make the gate fast" with
"make the gate mandatory."

It does not add a dependency, reach for GNU `parallel`, or introduce
`xargs -P`. Plain bash job control keeps the script portable and
matches the repository's existing scripting style.

It does not split the rust stream across multiple target directories.
That would take the critical path from ~205s to ~130s and the whole run
to ~2.2 min — see "Explicit cuts" for why it is recorded as the
highest-value follow-up rather than taken here.

## Pillar 1 — Every threshold is pre-registered, in this document, before its code exists

Inherited unchanged from `32UpdatePlan.md` and `33UpdatePlan.md`, whose
statement of the failure mode is not improved by rewording: "a
threshold chosen after seeing the data is a threshold fitted to the
data, and it will be chosen — consciously or not — to make the current
state pass."

`33UpdatePlan.md` added a clause this arc inherits and needs: **a root
cause offered for a missed threshold is itself a claim, and it gets a
measurement or it gets labelled a hypothesis.** That clause exists
because `32UpdatePlan.md` M8 explained a shortfall by reasoning from an
unmeasured premise about hardware, and was wrong. Every derivation in
this document's thresholds is stated inline at the milestone, and every
root cause offered in its running log carries a measurement.

## Pillar 2 — The script's verdict is the contract, and it is machine-checked by forcing a failure

Contract A says the script's output is preserved. The mechanism that
proves it is not inspection — it is deliberately breaking things.

The reason this pillar exists separately from the contract: for every
other arc in this series, a broken change announced itself. A moved
golden snapshot fails loudly. A nondeterministic generation fails
`determinism.rs` loudly. But a harness whose failure counting is broken
**passes everything, prints a reassuring message, and exits 0**. There
is no instrument in this repository that catches that except an
adversarial test.

So at every milestone boundary, without exception:

1. Run the script unmodified. It must exit 0 and report all
   combinations green.
2. Inject a failure — the pre-registered method is a temporary,
   reverted edit to one scenario's expected outcome, chosen because it
   exercises the `[FAIL]` parse path rather than the process-error
   path.
3. Confirm the script exits **non-zero**, names the right combination,
   and reports counts in the right units.
4. Inject a *process-level* failure instead (a combination whose `ciac
   sim` invocation errors outright), and confirm the same.
5. Revert both injections and re-confirm green.

Steps 2 and 4 are distinct on purpose: FM9 exists because those two
paths increment the same counter, and a rewrite that fixes one while
breaking the other would pass a single-path check.

### Contract D, the exact procedure

Left unspecified, "inject a failure" is the kind of instruction that
gets performed differently at each boundary and compared against
itself. Both recipes are fixed here so that every milestone's result is
comparable to every other milestone's.

**Injection A — the scenario path.** Pick one scenario file with a
deterministic expected outcome and perturb a single expected value in
it, so the simulator runs to completion and reports `[FAIL]`. The
pre-registered choice is `sim/quickstart.ciac-sim.json`, because
`quickstart` is the smallest program in the corpus (its rust
combination is the cheapest at ~44s cold, ~15s warm) and it carries
exactly one scenario, so the expected delta is unambiguous: exactly one
`[FAIL]` line, in exactly five combinations (one per target).

Expected outcome, stated in advance so a partial result is
recognizable as partial:

- exit code **non-zero**
- **5** failing scenarios (one per target — the same perturbed
  expectation fails identically everywhere, which is itself a small
  cross-target consistency check)
- **0** failing combinations (no process errored; they all ran and
  disagreed)
- the report names `quickstart.ciac` in five rows with `[FAIL]`

**Injection B — the process path.** Make one combination's `ciac sim`
invocation fail outright rather than disagree. The pre-registered
method is a temporary edit to the script's own `CIAC` variable path for
a single target's stream — pointing it at a nonexistent binary — which
fails at the process level without touching the corpus or any scenario
file, and without requiring a broken program to be committed anywhere
even temporarily.

Expected outcome:

- exit code **non-zero**
- **10** failing combinations (that target's ten programs)
- **0** failing scenarios (nothing ran far enough to emit `[PASS]` or
  `[FAIL]`)
- ten rows carrying the `(build/refusal)` marker

**Why both, every time.** Injection A exercises the parse-and-count
path; injection B exercises the process-error path. FM9 exists
precisely because the current script funnels both into one counter, so
a rewrite that correctly separates them must be shown to handle each —
and a rewrite that fixes one while breaking the other would pass either
check alone. The two expected outcomes above are also deliberately
*different in shape* (5 scenarios/0 combinations vs. 0 scenarios/10
combinations), so a tally that has silently merged the units again
cannot produce both.

**Both injections are reverted and green re-confirmed** before the
milestone is considered exited. The Contract D log in this document's
running-log section records both exit codes per boundary, so a reader
can confirm the check was run rather than asserted.

## Pillar 3 — Correctness before concurrency

M3 lands the accounting rewrite **serially**. M4 adds concurrency
afterward. The ordering is not negotiable and is the direct descendant
of `33UpdatePlan.md`'s Pillar 8 ("concurrency is pinned by a test
before it is introduced"), which itself existed because that arc had a
latent jar-write race that its own test-suite threading would have made
hot.

The reasoning is the same here in a different shape. The accounting
rewrite is the dangerous change; concurrency is the change that makes
the accounting rewrite's failure mode *reachable*. Landing them
together means a forced-failure test that fails cannot distinguish "the
tally is wrong" from "the concurrency lost a result," and the debugging
surface doubles at exactly the moment it matters most.

Landing them separately means M3's forced-failure proof is
unambiguous — the script is still serial, so any miscount is the
tally's fault — and M4 inherits a tally already proven correct.

## Pillar 4 — Two baselines, so no delta is ever ambiguous

- **`docs/perf/sim-corpus-baseline-v0.31.0.json`** (or the equivalent
  recorded section in this document's M1 entry, if a JSON artifact is
  judged heavier than the data warrants) — frozen at M1, never
  rewritten for the arc's duration. Every cumulative delta is measured
  against it.
- **The rolling per-milestone figures**, recorded in the running log,
  so each milestone's own incremental contribution is visible rather
  than only the accumulated total.

`33UpdatePlan.md`'s M1 discovered that its inherited baseline
disagreed with reality by 37% on the binary that mattered most, and
that four independent readings of the same quantity spanned 135s to
185s. **That is the direct precedent for this arc's measurement
methodology**, below, and for M1 taking two full-run reps rather than
trusting a single number.

## Pillar 5 — Rollback is graduated, and pre-registered

| Condition | Action |
|---|---|
| Any golden snapshot moves | **Revert.** Contract B breach. |
| `determinism.rs` fails | **Revert.** Contract B breach. |
| The script reports green on a forced failure | **Revert immediately.** Contract D breach — this is the arc's defining failure and carries no "disclose and keep" option. |
| The script's `[PASS]`/`[FAIL]` set changes | **Revert.** Contract A breach. |
| Orphaned processes survive an interrupt | **Revert.** FM4 unresolved. |
| Any unrelated metric regresses beyond its noise band | **Revert**, unless the trade is stated and accepted in the milestone report. |
| Gain < ½ the pre-registered threshold | **Revert.** The complexity is not paid for. |
| Gain ≥ ½ but < the threshold | **Keep**, record the shortfall explicitly, and **measure the proposed root cause before recording it** (Pillar 1). |
| Gain ≥ threshold | **Keep.** |

**On ratio-shaped thresholds** (M2's `≥2×`, M4's `≥2.5×`): "gain" means
the speedup achieved over baseline, and a ratio threshold's zero point
is 1.0×, not 0. The half-threshold point for a `≥T×` threshold is
`(T + 1) / 2` — so **1.5× for M2** and **1.75× for M4**. This
formulation is inherited from `33UpdatePlan.md`'s Pillar 5, which added
it mid-arc after an earlier draft stated a half-point of "0.9×" for a
speedup ratio, which is incoherent (below 1.0× is a slowdown, not half
a gain).

Note the third row has no graduated option. Every other failure in this
table admits a "keep and disclose" reading at some magnitude; a harness
that passes when it should fail does not, because the magnitude of that
defect is not measurable in percent.

## Pillar 6 — The parallelism axis is a correctness decision, not a performance one

Choosing to parallelize across **targets** rather than **programs** is
what makes FM6 and FM8 resolvable at all, and the document records it
as a design constraint that later work must not casually revisit.

Under the target axis, each toolchain has exactly one concurrent user:
one cargo, one npm, one go, one maven, one uv. Their respective caches
(`CARGO_TARGET_DIR`, the npm cache, `GOCACHE`/`GOMODCACHE`, `~/.m2`,
the uv cache) are each touched by a single process at a time, which is
the same access pattern they see today under the fully serial loop.

Under the program axis, all five toolchains would run five-way
concurrently. Maven's local repository is the best-known hazard there,
and cargo would serialize on lever 1's shared target-dir lock — turning
the two levers into partial opponents rather than multipliers.

If a future arc wants more parallelism than `max(stream)` allows, the
correct move is **more streams within a target** (the two-target-dirs
idea under "Explicit cuts"), not abandoning the axis.

## Pillar 7 — The checkpoint has real teeth, including the authority to stop

M6 is a hard serialization point: no work under M7 or M8 lands before
M6's own commit does.

Its four pre-registered outcomes:

- **(a) Proceed.** Cumulative gain clears the arc's headline, Contract
  D holds, no other contract is breached. M7 and M8 run as written.
- **(b) Proceed narrowed.** One lever underperformed or one failure
  mode proved unresolvable as designed; that piece is reverted and the
  rest ships. M7's documentation adjusts to describe what actually

- **(c) Stop and ship what exists.** If M2 alone delivers most of the

  complexity in a script with no other parallelism. Recorded as a

- **(d) Stop and revert.** Contract D cannot be satisfied under
  concurrency despite M5's hardening. The measured result and the
  evidence go in the running log, and the arc closes having established
  that this script resists safe parallelization — which is a real
  finding, not a failure.

## Pillar 8 — Reuse before invention

Lever 1's entire design is "use the helper that already exists."
`shared_cargo_target_dir()` and `apply_validate_env` are not merely
convenient; they already encode two decisions this arc would otherwise
have to re-make and could get wrong: *where* the shared directory lives
(a stable path in `temp_dir()`, so it survives across runs and
processes) and *when not to apply it* (never override a caller-set
`CARGO_TARGET_DIR`).

The tempting alternative — exporting `CARGO_TARGET_DIR` in the bash
script — was rejected, and the reason is instructive enough to record
as a pillar rather than a footnote: it would have been **more code and
more dangerous**, redirecting the bootstrap `cargo build -p ciac`
(`:31`) and leaving `CIAC="./target/debug/ciac"` (`:32`) pointing at a
stale or absent binary. Reaching for the existing abstraction did not
just save effort; it dissolved a failure mode (FM11) that the
hand-rolled version would have had to defend against.

## Measurement methodology

A 13-minute script is expensive to measure, and the temptation to
under-sample is the single most likely way this arc produces a number
it should not trust. `33UpdatePlan.md` caught contaminated readings
three separate times — most consequentially at its own M1, where four
independent readings of the same quantity spanned 135s to 185s and the
committed baseline turned out to be 37% high on the binary that
mattered most. This arc inherits that lesson as binding procedure
rather than advice.

**Isolation.** Nothing else runs on the machine during a timing run.
Not a build, not a test suite, not another shell doing something
harmless. This session observed a ~50% inflation on two test binaries
measured while a background `sim-corpus` run was still in flight — the
readings were discarded and retaken rather than reported.

**Disk.** Free space is checked and brought below 80% utilization
before any timing run. Disk pressure changes toolchain behavior
(cargo's and npm's in particular) in ways that look like performance
noise but are not.

**Repetitions.** Two for full runs, three for anything cheaper. The
2-rep choice is a **deliberate, disclosed deviation** from this series'
3-rep norm, justified by each full run costing ~13 minutes at baseline;
it is recorded at M1 as a deviation rather than quietly taken. Where a
decision turns on a margin narrower than the observed spread between
the two reps, M6 takes a third rather than deciding on two.

**Decomposition.** Per-target figures come from five separate
`--targets <one>` runs rather than from instrumenting the script. They
sum to the same total work, they need no code change to obtain, and
they give exactly the stream-level numbers M4's threshold is measured
against. This is the same reasoning that made `--targets` worth having
in the first place.

**What is compared to what.** M2's threshold is a per-program rust
figure (cold vs. warm cache). M4's is a whole-run figure against M3's
whole-run figure — deliberately *not* against M1's, because M3 already
contains lever 1 and comparing M4 to M1 would credit parallelism with
caching's win. Every ratio in the running log states both of its
operands.

## The failure-mode register

Lever 2 is where this arc's risk lives, and the register is how that
risk is discharged. Each entry states the mechanism, the resolution,
**how completely it is resolved**, and how the resolution is verified.
The completeness labels are load-bearing and honest: one entry is
*mitigated* rather than eliminated, and saying so plainly is the point
of having a register instead of a reassuring paragraph.

### FM1 — Lost failure counts (the dangerous one)

**Mechanism.** `FAILED` and `TOTAL` (`:61-62`) are shell variables
incremented inside the loop (`:74`, `:79`, `:86`). Backgrounding the
loop body places those increments in a subshell, which receives a
*copy* of the parent's variables; the increments are discarded when the
subshell exits. The parent then evaluates `[[ "$FAILED" -eq 0 ]]`
(`:103`) against a value that never moved, prints "all combinations
green," and exits 0 — while combinations were failing. **This is the
failure mode that would make the script actively harmful rather than
merely slow.**

**Resolution — by construction.** No counter crosses a process boundary
at all. Each combination writes two files into a results directory: its
captured output, and its exit code. The tally is computed after `wait`,
in the parent, by reading those files. The failure mode cannot occur
because the mechanism it exploits has been removed rather than guarded.

```bash
run_combination() {                       # runs inside a stream subshell
    local program=$1 target=$2 slot=$3
    local out="$WORKDIR/$(basename "$program" .ciac)-$target"
    local scenario_args=() s
    for s in ${PROGRAM_SCENARIOS[$program]}; do
        scenario_args+=(--scenario "$s")
    done

    rm -rf "$out"
    local rc=0 result
    result=$("$CIAC" sim --target "$target" --out "$out" \
                 "${scenario_args[@]}" "$program" 2>&1) || rc=$?
    printf '%s\n' "$result" >"$RESULTS/$slot.out"
    printf '%d\n'  "$rc"    >"$RESULTS/$slot.rc"
    rm -rf "$out"
}
```

**Verification.** M3's forced-failure test on both injection paths, and
Contract D at every subsequent milestone boundary.

### FM2 — Background exit codes never reach the parent

**Mechanism.** `set -e` does not fire for a failing background job, and
a bare `wait` returns the status of the last job only. A stream that
died — from an unexpected signal, an OOM kill, or a bug in the stream
function itself — would go entirely unnoticed, and worse, would leave
its combinations' status files missing rather than failing, so a
naive tally that counts only the files present would report green.

**Resolution — entirely.** Track every stream PID in an array, wait on
each individually, and capture status explicitly rather than relying on
`set -e`. Additionally, the tally asserts that the **expected number of
status files exists** — a missing slot is itself a failure, which
closes the "died before writing" gap that PID-waiting alone would not:

```bash
pids=()
for target in "${TARGET_LIST[@]}"; do
    run_target_stream "$target" &
    pids+=("$!")
done

stream_failure=0
for pid in "${pids[@]}"; do
    wait "$pid" || stream_failure=1
done

# A slot with no .rc file means its stream died before recording a
# result -- that is a failure, not an absence.
for slot in "${ALL_SLOTS[@]}"; do
    [[ -f "$RESULTS/$slot.rc" ]] || {
        printf 'missing result for %s\n' "$slot" >&2
        stream_failure=1
    }
done
```

**Verification.** M5 kills a stream mid-flight and confirms the parent
reports failure.

### FM3 — Output interleaving

**Mechanism.** Five concurrent streams writing `printf` lines to one
stdout produce interleaved and potentially line-torn output, making the
report unreadable and its ordering nondeterministic between runs.

**Resolution — entirely, and an improvement on today.** Each
combination's output is buffered to its own file (FM1's mechanism
already does this), and the parent renders the report after `wait` by
iterating slots in deterministic sorted order. Because today's ordering
is hash-based — verified: `sim-peripherals, quickstart,
sim-three-service, query-verbs, order-system` — the result is *more*
deterministic than the current script, and this arc claims that as a
small improvement rather than defending it as mere preservation.

**Verification.** M4's ≥3-consecutive-identical-reports exit criterion.

### FM4 — Orphaned grandchildren, and a trap that races live writers

**Mechanism.** Two distinct problems from the same root. First, the
existing trap is `trap 'rm -rf "$WORKDIR"' EXIT` (`:29`) — `EXIT` only,
not `INT` or `TERM`. Under concurrency, a Ctrl-C at the parent fires
that trap and deletes `WORKDIR` while five streams are still writing
into it. Second, and worse: killing a stream subshell does **not** kill
its grandchildren. The `cargo`, `npm`, `mvnw`, and `go` processes it
spawned keep running, orphaned, holding CPU and disk — and on this
4-vCPU machine, five orphaned toolchain builds are enough to make the
developer's next action unusably slow with no visible cause.

**Resolution — entirely, via process groups.** Enable job control with
`set -m` before launching streams. In a non-interactive bash shell,
this causes each background job to become a **process-group leader**,
which is what makes group signalling possible. The cleanup handler then
signals the whole group with `kill -- -"$pid"` (the negative PID form),
reaping grandchildren, waits for them, and only then removes
`WORKDIR`. The trap is widened to `EXIT INT TERM`:

```bash
set -m    # each background job becomes its own process-group leader,
          # which is what makes `kill -- -PID` reach grandchildren

cleanup() {
    local pid
    for pid in "${pids[@]:-}"; do
        kill -- -"$pid" 2>/dev/null || true
    done
    for pid in "${pids[@]:-}"; do
        wait "$pid" 2>/dev/null || true
    done
    rm -rf "$WORKDIR"
}
trap cleanup EXIT INT TERM
```

`set -m`'s effect is the least widely known construct in this design
and must carry that comment at the call site rather than being left for
a reader to infer.

**Verification.** M5 interrupts a live run and confirms both that no
toolchain process survives and that `WORKDIR` is gone.

### FM5 — Disk exhaustion

**Mechanism.** Five `$out` directories alive concurrently instead of
one. This script has hit disk exhaustion before — its own comment
(`:92-97`) records that an early run "hit exactly that on its last
combination," which is why the `rm -rf` on `:98` exists.

**Resolution — mitigated and measured, not eliminated.** Three parts.
The per-combination `rm -rf` is retained exactly as it stands, so at
most five `$out` directories are ever live. Lever 1's shared cargo
directory replaces ten sequential multi-GB per-project trees with one
~2.3 GB tree, so the expected net effect on peak disk is a *reduction*
— M5 measures this against M1's serial high-water mark rather than
assuming it. And a pre-flight free-space check fails early with a clear
message rather than mid-run with a confusing toolchain error.

**This entry is deliberately not claimed as resolved entirely.** Disk
is a shared, finite resource that another process on the same machine
can consume at any moment; no amount of care inside this script makes
exhaustion impossible. An honest register says so, and
`32UpdatePlan.md` M8 and `33UpdatePlan.md` M4 both recorded real
disk-exhaustion incidents during their own execution — this is not a
hypothetical.

**Verification.** M5's constrained-free-space run, plus the measured
concurrent high-water mark.

### FM6 — Toolchain cache concurrency

**Mechanism.** Five toolchains touching `~/.m2`, the npm cache,
`GOCACHE`/`GOMODCACHE`, and the uv cache simultaneously. Maven's local
repository is the best-documented hazard under concurrent writes.

**Resolution — by design, through the choice of axis.** Parallelizing
by target gives each toolchain exactly **one** concurrent user — the
same access pattern each cache sees today under the serial loop. See
Pillar 6 for why this is treated as a correctness decision rather than
a performance one, and why a future arc wanting more parallelism should
add streams *within* a target rather than abandon the axis.

**Verification.** Structural — it follows from the axis. M4's
≥3-identical-runs criterion would surface cache corruption as report
divergence if the reasoning were wrong.

### FM7 — Wall-timeout trips under contention

**Mechanism.** Individual combinations run slower under five-way
contention and could exceed a wall-clock timeout they would clear
serially, turning a speedup into intermittent failure.

**Resolution — does not exist.** Verified: the CLI path passes
`wall_timeout: None` (`commands.rs:2459`, `:2498`); the 60-second
`MCP_SIM_WALL_TIMEOUT` (`:2524`) is used only by the MCP path
(`:2567`). Recorded in the register rather than omitted, because "we
checked and it isn't a problem" is more useful to a future reader than
silence.

### FM8 — Cargo target-dir lock contention

**Mechanism.** Concurrent cargo invocations against lever 1's shared
directory would serialize on cargo's lock, partially cancelling lever
2's benefit.

**Resolution — entirely.** Under target-axis parallelism exactly one
rust stream exists, so there is no concurrent cargo at all. Even if
there were, `shared_cargo_target_dir()`'s own doc comment
(`:3739-3741`) documents concurrent use as safe rather than merely
serialized.

### FM9 — Pre-existing accounting bug: two units in one counter

**Mechanism.** `FAILED` is incremented once per errored *combination*
(`:79`) and once per failing *scenario* line (`:86`), while `TOTAL`
counts only combinations (`:74`). The closing message (`:106`) reads
"`$FAILED` failing combination(s) out of `$TOTAL`" — comparing two
different units. A combination that both errors and emits `[FAIL]`
lines is double-counted, and because several programs carry multiple
scenarios (`sim-peripherals` has four), the reported failure count can
exceed the number of combinations that exist.

**Resolution — entirely.** Track two distinct counters, failed
combinations and failed scenarios, and report both with correct units.
This is a pre-existing defect that this arc fixes as a side effect; it
is in scope because M3 rewrites the tally regardless, and leaving a
known miscount in place while rewriting the code around it would be
indefensible.

**Verification.** M3's two-path forced-failure test, which is
specifically designed so that a rewrite fixing one path while breaking
the other cannot pass.

### FM10 — A `set -e` landmine in the parse loop

**Mechanism.** `[[ "$mark" == "[FAIL]" ]] && FAILED=$((FAILED + 1))`
(`:86`) is an AND-list whose first command fails on every `[PASS]`
line. It works today, but it depends on subtle `set -e` exemption rules
for AND-lists and is fragile against future edits.

**Resolution — entirely.** Convert to an explicit `if`. Recorded as a
robustness improvement rather than a bug fix, since the current form is
not observably broken — overstating it would be its own small
dishonesty.

### FM11 — Bootstrap redirect

**Mechanism.** Exporting `CARGO_TARGET_DIR` in the script — the obvious
way to implement lever 1 without touching Rust — would also redirect
the bootstrap `cargo build -p ciac` (`:31`), after which
`CIAC="./target/debug/ciac"` (`:32`) would point at a stale binary or
nothing at all. The script would then either fail confusingly or,
worse, silently test a previously-built `ciac` rather than the current
one.

**Resolution — dissolved.** The CLI-side decision (Pillar 8) means the
script sets no environment variable at all, so the bootstrap build is
untouched. The failure mode ceases to exist rather than being guarded
against — which is the strongest form of resolution available and the
clearest argument for having chosen the CLI over the script.

### The shape of the rewritten script

The register's individual resolutions compose into one structure. It is
sketched here so the implementer is not assembling it from eleven
separate fragments, and so a reviewer can see that the pieces fit.

```bash
set -euo pipefail
set -m                        # background jobs become process-group
                              # leaders -- required for FM4's
                              # `kill -- -PID` to reach grandchildren

WORKDIR=$(mktemp -d)
RESULTS="$WORKDIR/results"    # one .out + one .rc per combination
mkdir -p "$RESULTS"
pids=()

cleanup() {                   # FM4
    local pid
    for pid in "${pids[@]:-}"; do kill -- -"$pid" 2>/dev/null || true; done
    for pid in "${pids[@]:-}"; do wait "$pid"      2>/dev/null || true; done
    rm -rf "$WORKDIR"
}
trap cleanup EXIT INT TERM

preflight_disk_check          # FM5 -- hard-fail with required free space

cargo build -p ciac >/dev/null    # untouched: FM11 does not exist here
CIAC="./target/debug/ciac"

# --- one stream per target; each stream is serial internally --------
run_target_stream() {         # FM6: exactly one user per toolchain
    local target=$1 program
    for program in "${SORTED_PROGRAMS[@]}"; do
        run_combination "$program" "$target" "$(slot_id "$program" "$target")"
    done
}

for target in "${TARGET_LIST[@]}"; do
    run_target_stream "$target" &
    pids+=("$!")
done

# --- join, then tally in the parent ---------------------------------
stream_failure=0
for pid in "${pids[@]}"; do   # FM2: explicit per-PID wait
    wait "$pid" || stream_failure=1
done

failed_combinations=0         # FM9: two counters, two units
failed_scenarios=0
total_combinations=0

print_header
for slot in "${ALL_SLOTS[@]}"; do          # FM3: deterministic order
    [[ -f "$RESULTS/$slot.rc" ]] || {      # FM2: a missing slot is a failure
        printf 'missing result for %s\n' "$slot" >&2
        stream_failure=1
        continue
    }
    render_and_tally "$slot"               # FM1: parent-side counting
done

report_and_exit               # FM9: both counts, correct units
```

Three properties of this shape are worth stating because they are what
make the register's claims true rather than aspirational:

1. **No counter is ever incremented outside the parent.** `run_combination`
   writes files; only `render_and_tally` counts. FM1 is impossible by
   construction rather than by care.
2. **Absence is a failure, not a zero.** The `[[ -f ... ]]` guard means
   a stream that dies before recording cannot be mistaken for a stream
   with nothing to report — which is the gap that per-PID waiting alone
   would leave.
3. **Iteration is over `ALL_SLOTS`, not over whatever files exist.**
   The expected set is computed up front from the corpus, so the tally
   is driven by what *should* have happened, not by what did.

## Open questions resolved at implementation (pre-registered)

Recorded now, with their decision rules, so the answers cannot be
reverse-engineered from whatever turns out to be convenient.

1. **Does `ciac sim` share `ciac verify`'s target directory, or get its
   own?** Resolved in advance: **share it.** The dependency trees are
   identical — same template, same pins — so one directory strictly
   maximizes hit rate, and a developer who runs `ciac verify` then
   `ciac sim` (or the reverse) gets a warm cache for free. The
   directory's name (`ciac-verify-cargo-target`) becomes mildly
   inaccurate; the helper's doc comment is updated to say it serves
   both commands rather than renaming the path, because renaming would
   orphan every existing developer's warm cache for a cosmetic gain.
   **Reversal rule:** if sharing produces any measurable interference —
   a `verify` run invalidating a `sim` cache or vice versa, visible as
   a warm run costing cold time — the two split, and the split is
   recorded as a deviation.

2. **Should the forced-failure test become a committed check?**
   Deliberately **not decided** by this arc. It runs as a procedure at
   every milestone boundary (Pillar 2). Making it a committed
   `sim-corpus-selftest.sh` would be a new permanent artifact testing a
   shell script's process handling, and this arc has no soak behind
   that decision. Recorded as the top candidate for the *next* arc's
   inventory, with the observation that its value is highest precisely
   because the property it checks is invisible to every other
   instrument.

3. **What worker count, if a future arc adds streams within a target?**
   Not applicable to this arc — five targets means five streams, fixed
   by the corpus. Pre-registered anyway because the "Explicit cuts"
   entry invites it: any within-target split must keep one cargo
   process per target directory, so N streams require N target
   directories, and the disk cost (~2.3 GB each) is the binding
   constraint rather than CPU.

4. **What happens if a target is added to the corpus?** The design
   scales without change: one more stream, one more toolchain with one
   concurrent user. Recorded so that a future target addition does not
   trigger a redesign out of caution.

5. **Does the pre-flight disk check hard-fail or warn?** Resolved:
   **hard-fail**, with the required free space stated in the message.
   A warning that scrolls past during a 3-minute run is a warning
   nobody reads, and the failure it precedes is a confusing
   mid-toolchain error rather than a clear one. **Rule:** the threshold
   is set from M5's measured concurrent high-water mark plus a stated
   margin, not guessed.

## Verification strategy

- **Contract D at every milestone boundary**, both injection paths.
  This is the arc's primary verification mechanism and the one with the
  weakest natural incentive to perform, which is why it is stated as
  absolute rather than left to judgment.
- **The script's own report, diffed against M1's baseline**, at every
  boundary. Contract A is mechanically checkable: the `[PASS]`/`[FAIL]`
  set either matches or it does not.
- **≥3 consecutive identical reports** from M4 onward. A parallel
  harness that varies between runs has failed Contract A even when
  every individual run is green.
- **Full `cargo test --workspace`** at every boundary, including
  script-only milestones — see the exit checklist for why.
- **`ciac-bench --compare` in full at M2 and M6** (Contract C), owed
  because M2 touches compiler code.
- **`perf_baseline.rs` / `perf_scaling.rs` expected flat.** No Rust
  path either measures is changed; movement means something unintended
  happened.
- **Adversarial verification at M5** for every register row.

The end-to-end sequence, runnable as written:

## Milestone dependencies and parallelism

```
M1 ──▶ M2 ──▶ M3 ──▶ M4 ──▶ M5 ──▶ M6 (CHECKPOINT) ──▶ M7 ──▶ M8
        │             ▲
        └─────────────┘
     (M2 is independent of M3-M5 in content,
      but ordered first so M4's threshold is
      measured against a lever-1 baseline)
```

**M3 must precede M4, and this is the arc's one non-negotiable
ordering.** Pillar 3 states the reasoning: the accounting rewrite is
the dangerous change, concurrency is what makes its failure mode
reachable, and landing them together makes a failed forced-failure test
ambiguous between the two.

**M2 is independent of M3–M5 in content** — it touches Rust, they touch
bash — and could in principle land in either order. It is placed first
so that M4's `≥2.5×` threshold is measured against a baseline that
already includes lever 1, which is the number a reader actually wants:
"what did parallelism add, on top of caching."

**M5 must follow M4** because three of its five adversarial checks
(stream kill, interrupt-with-orphans, concurrent disk high-water mark)
have no meaning against a serial script.

**M6 is a hard serialization point** with the authority to stop the arc
(Pillar 7).

## Explicit cuts

- **No splitting the rust stream across multiple target directories.**
  This is the highest-value follow-up and it is measured, not guessed:
  two shared target dirs with five rust programs each would let both
  warm independently, taking the critical path from ~205s to ~130s and
  the whole run from ~3.4 min to **~2.2 min**. Cut here because it
  costs a second ~2.3 GB tree (FM5's binding constraint) and introduces
  a within-target scheduling scheme with no in-repo precedent, on top
  of an arc that is already establishing the repository's first
  bash-parallelism pattern. **Recorded with its numbers so the next arc
  need not re-derive it.**
- **No parallelism on the program axis.** Explicitly rejected — it
  breaks FM6's and FM8's resolutions. See Pillar 6.
- **No changes to the corpus.** Which programs, which scenarios, and
  which pairings are all untouched. This arc makes the same 50
  combinations faster; it does not make them fewer.
- **No `--jobs`/`-j` flag.** The stream count is fixed by the target
  count, so a knob would only let a user *under*-subscribe, which the
  existing `--targets` filter already provides more usefully.
- **No GNU `parallel`, no `xargs -P`, no new dependency.**
- **No CI wiring.** See "What this arc does not do."
- **No caching of non-rust toolchains.** TypeScript's `npm ci`
  reinstalls `node_modules` per project and is the second-largest cost
  at 9.8s, but its cache is already shared via `~/.npm` and the
  remaining cost is per-project installation that a shared cache cannot
  remove. Not attempted; the ~13% share does not justify the risk of
  sharing `node_modules` trees between generated projects.

## Risks

- **This arc establishes the repository's first bash-parallelism
  pattern.** Verified: no `&`, `wait`, or `xargs -P` anywhere under
  `scripts/`. There is no in-repo precedent to copy, no established
  house idiom to follow, and no second example a reviewer can compare
  against. Mitigated by M3 and M5 existing as separate milestones
  rather than being folded into M4, and by every construct carrying an
  explanatory comment at its call site.
- **A silently-passing harness is the worst available outcome**, and is
  exactly what a careless FM1 implementation produces. Contract D is
  the mitigation and it is absolute — Pillar 5's table gives that row
  no graduated option.
- **`set -m` in a non-interactive script is obscure.** Its behavior —
  background jobs become process-group leaders, which is what makes
  `kill -- -PID` reach grandchildren — is not common knowledge and must
  be commented where it is set, not left for a reader to infer.
- **Disk remains a shared finite resource** (FM5), mitigated and
  measured rather than eliminated. Two prior arcs in this series hit
  real disk-exhaustion incidents during execution.
- **M2 touches compiler code**, so unlike the script-only milestones it
  owes the full byte-identity apparatus and the full `ciac-bench`
  sweep, not just a green sim-corpus run.
- **The 2-rep measurement methodology is thinner than this series'
  norm.** Justified by cost (each full run is ~13 minutes) and
  disclosed at M1 rather than taken quietly, but it does mean this
  arc's headline numbers carry wider error bars than
  `33UpdatePlan.md`'s 3-rep medians. Where a decision turns on a small
  margin, M6 takes a third rep rather than deciding on two.
- **Every ratio here was measured on 4 vCPUs.** On a 16-core machine
  lever 2's benefit would be larger (streams would contend less) while
  lever 1's would be unchanged (it is I/O and compilation work, not
  parallelism). Ratios travel; absolutes do not.

## Prior art in this series, and what this arc inherits

Each of the three preceding arcs contributed something this one uses
directly, and naming the lineage keeps a reader from mistaking
inherited discipline for invention.

**From `31UpdatePlan.md` (the instrument arc, 0.29.0)** — the habit of
characterizing noise before trusting a number. That arc measured this
sandbox's wall-clock relative standard deviation at 68.1% against real
CI's 11.6%, the opposite of the usual assumption, and concluded that
**every wall-clock metric in this repository is permanently
reporting-only** rather than gateable. This arc's headline metric is a
wall clock, so that conclusion applies in full: nothing here becomes a
committed gate, the thresholds are decision rules for a human executing
milestones, and the measurement methodology section exists because that
arc proved it has to.

**From `32UpdatePlan.md` (the first optimization arc, 0.30.0)** — two
things. The shared `CARGO_TARGET_DIR` mechanism itself, which its M8
item 6 introduced for `ciac verify` and which lever 1 now simply
extends to `ciac sim`; this arc writes no new caching logic because
that arc already wrote it. And, less comfortably, the cautionary tale:
its M8 item 7 recorded a root cause it had never measured, and was
wrong. Pillar 1's clause requiring a measurement behind every offered
root cause is a direct descendant of that mistake.

**From `33UpdatePlan.md` (the correction arc, 0.31.0)** — the
structural discipline this arc leans on hardest. Its Pillar 8
("concurrency is pinned by a test before it is introduced") is the
direct ancestor of Pillar 3 and of the M3-before-M4 ordering. Its
graduated-rollback table, including the ratio-threshold half-point
formulation `(T + 1) / 2`, is inherited verbatim. Its M1 — which found
four readings of one quantity spanning 135s to 185s and a committed
baseline 37% high — is why this arc takes two full-run reps and states
its isolation rules as binding. And its M6 produced the finding that
makes lever 1's ceiling what it is: the AppCDS archive that cut the
Java formatter's fixed cost, which is why java sits at 7.5s in the
opening table rather than the ~11s it would otherwise cost.

**What this arc contributes back.** Contract D is new — no prior arc
needed a contract asserting that its own instrument could still fail,
because no prior arc changed an instrument's failure-reporting
machinery. Pillar 6 (the parallelism axis as a correctness decision) is
new. Pillar 8 (reuse before invention) formalizes something the
previous arcs practiced without naming. And the failure-mode register
as a first-class numbered artifact with per-entry completeness labels
is a format this arc introduces, because it is the first one whose risk
is concentrated in a single silent failure shape rather than spread
across twelve optimization items.

## Confidence and handoff

This document is written whole, before M1's own commit, per the
standing precedent that a plan is authored and then executed milestone
by milestone rather than discovered as it goes. Every threshold above
was chosen before the code that must clear it exists.

The arc is deliberately small in footprint — one Rust function pair,
one shell script, and documentation — while being unusually heavy in
verification discipline for its size. That asymmetry is intentional and
worth stating plainly: the code is easy and the failure mode is
invisible, which is the exact combination that produces a confident,
broken result. Five of the eight milestones exist primarily to make
sure the thing still works, and only two of them make it faster.

The handoff to the next arc is three things. First, a fast enough
correctness gate that running it stops being a decision — 3.4 minutes
is short enough to run on every meaningful change, which is worth more
than the raw time saved. Second, a measured, un-taken opportunity: the
two-target-dir split, with its numbers attached (~2.2 min), cut for
disk and precedent reasons that a future arc may weigh differently.
Third, an open question deliberately left open — whether the
forced-failure test should become a committed self-check — recorded
with the observation that makes it interesting: it is the only property
in this repository that no existing instrument can see.

**Second M1 finding, from Contract D's Injection A (see the Contract D
log below): FM9's double-count is not an edge case, it is the normal
case.** The plan's Injection A recipe assumed a perturbed scenario
expectation would produce "0 failing combinations, 5 failing scenarios"
— i.e., that `ciac sim` would report the mismatch in-band via `[FAIL]`
while still exiting 0. Measured behavior is different: `ciac sim` exits
**non-zero** on *any* failed scenario expectation, while *also* emitting
a `[FAIL]` diagnostic line naming the mismatch. Against the unmodified
script this means the ERROR branch (`:78-79`) and the `[FAIL]`-line
branch (`:86`) both fire for the same underlying failure on **every**
scenario mismatch, not merely "a combination that both errors and emits
`[FAIL]` lines" as an occasional overlap. Verified directly: all five
`quickstart` target rows carried both a `[FAIL]` line and a
`(build/refusal)` ERROR line, and the script's own tally read exactly
**10** — 5 targets × 2 increments each — matching this mechanism exactly.
This *strengthens* the case for M3's FM9 fix (two separate, correctly
scoped counters) rather than changing it: the defect is guaranteed to
fire on the harness's single most common failure shape, not a rare
combination of two independent failure modes.

## Retrospective

Written at M8, against M1's frozen baseline (mean 1305.4s across two
reps, itself ~1.7× this document's own pre-code estimate — see M1's
note for the reconciliation).

**Cumulative wall-time delta and each lever's separate contribution.**
Final measured state: 949.3s (M8's own close-out run), consistent
with M6's 929.4s within normal noise — **1.38–1.40× against M1**,
**all of it lever 1**. Lever 1 (M2, shared cargo target directory)
delivered its full, verified gain: 3.07× rust steady-state, byte-
identical results cold vs. warm-shared. Lever 2 (M4, target-axis
parallelism) contributed **zero** to the shipped state — implemented
correctly, measured at 1.64× against its own ≥2.5× threshold, and
reverted per Pillar 5 before any of its gain could be banked. The
arc's headline number is therefore **1.4×, not the pre-registered
2.5–3.4× range** — a real, disclosed shortfall against the document's
own ambition, not a failure to execute what was planned.

**Thresholds: met, missed-but-kept, or reverted.** M2 — **met** (3.07×
≥ 2×, comfortably). M4 — **reverted** (1.64× against ≥2.5×, below the
1.75× half-point; Pillar 5's table has no "missed-but-kept" reading
for a gain that low, and none was taken). No milestone landed in the
"missed-but-kept" band at all — the arc's two ratio-shaped thresholds
were either cleared outright or missed decisively enough to trigger
the table's other branch. M1, M3, M6, M7, M8 carried no threshold by
design (baseline, correctness, checkpoint, documentation, close-out
respectively) and all five passed their own stated exit criteria.

**Did Contract D catch something the timing numbers did not?** Twice,
and both were real findings, not procedural formalities. First, at
M1: the pre-registered Injection A recipe assumed `ciac sim` would
report a mismatched expectation in-band (`[FAIL]`) while still exiting
0 — timing measurements never would have surfaced this assumption's
falseness. The actual injection revealed `ciac sim` exits non-zero on
*any* failed expectation, meaning FM9's double-count is the normal
case for a scenario mismatch, not a rare overlap — a correction to
this document's own understanding, caught before M3's fix was
designed around it, not after. Second, at M3: the sorted-diff
Contract A check (part of the same discipline, run alongside every
Contract D pass) caught a real bug — `slot_id()` stripped the `.ciac`
extension, changing the report's PROGRAM column text though not its
verdicts — that no wall-clock measurement would ever have surfaced,
since the script's timing and exit code were both correct throughout.

**Did the register's completeness labels survive M5's adversarial
verification?** They were never tested against it, and that is itself
the honest answer. M5's adversarial pass — kill a stream mid-flight,
interrupt the whole run, constrain free space — requires concurrency
to exist; M4 was reverted before M5 ran, so the eleven-entry
failure-mode register's labels (ten "entirely"/"by construction"/"by
design", one "mitigated") remain as-designed claims verified by
direct code reasoning and the green-path tests that did run (Contract
A's sorted-diff, the 2-target smoke test), not by the adversarial
procedure this document specified for them. FM5's "mitigated" label in
particular was never stress-tested with a constrained-disk run — it is
therefore neither confirmed too optimistic nor too pessimistic, it is
**untested**, and this document says so rather than letting an unrun
milestone's silence be mistaken for validation. This is the most
honest thing to report from an arc whose second lever did not ship:
a register built for a mechanism that was never turned on stays a
well-reasoned design document, not a verified one.

**Did the 2-rep methodology prove sufficient, or need widening at
M6?** Sufficient, and not widened. M1's own 2 reps (disclosed
deviation from this series' 3-rep norm) agreed within 131.6s (10% of
their mean) and were corroborated by a third data point from Contract
D's own green re-confirmation. M4's decision-critical 2 reps (613.3s,
593.5s) agreed within 3% of each other and landed 5-9% past the
half-point line in the same direction — nowhere near a margin
requiring the "M6 takes a third" escape hatch this document's own
measurement methodology built in for close calls. M6's own
re-measurement was a single confirming rep, not a fresh 2-rep
baseline, because the state under test was already proven
byte-identical to M3's thrice-measured serial state.

**Did any root cause offered in this document's running log get
reached before its measurement, rather than after?** No — every root
cause stated in this log was measured, and one place this document
deliberately declined to over-claim is itself evidence of the
discipline holding: M1's ~1.7× wall-clock inflation against the
pre-code estimate was measured (isolation confirmed, disk checked,
`user`/`real` ratio characterized) but its *exact* mechanism was left
an open, disclosed question rather than assigned an unmeasured cause —
the opposite failure to `32UpdatePlan.md` M8's own mistake, which this
series' Pillar 1 clause exists to prevent. M4's CPU-oversubscription
diagnosis was reached only after comparing rust's isolated stream time
(M2's own 380.89s) against the whole-run time under concurrency, not
asserted from the ratio miss alone. M2's Contract C noise finding was
reached only after a same-session stash/unstash comparison, not
accepted from the first, misleading committed-baseline comparison.

**What this arc hands off.** A shipped, verified 1.4× win at low risk
and low complexity (lever 1). A correctly-implemented, honestly
reverted 1.64× mechanism (lever 2) with its numbers, its root cause,
and the specific hardware constraint (4 physical cores) that bounded
it, all on record for a future arc — either on hardware where the core
count no longer binds, or via the bounded-concurrency alternative
named during this arc's own execution (capping simultaneous streams
below the core count, or prioritizing rust's build over the four
cheaper streams, rather than launching all five unconditionally) as a
materially different mechanism deserving its own pre-registered
threshold rather than a rescue of this one. And an unresolved question
this document leaves open rather than answers by default: whether the
eleven-entry failure-mode register, never adversarially exercised, is
sound design or merely untested design — the next arc that ships
concurrency for this script inherits the obligation to find out.
